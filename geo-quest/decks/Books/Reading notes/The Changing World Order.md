title: The Changing World Order

Ray Dalio, Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order: Why Nations Succeed and Fail (2021). Dalio, founder of the hedge fund Bridgewater Associates, studied the rise and fall of the leading empires of the last 500 years to understand what's coming now.
The argument: history runs in a Big Cycle, roughly a lifetime long. Empires rise in a period of building after a new order, peak in excesses of debt and wealth gaps, then decline through internal conflict and a fight with a rising rival, until a new order begins. Three cycles drive it: money and debt, internal order and disorder, and external order and disorder. Today's version: the United States at the top with a lot of debt and deep divisions, and China rising.
The book: Part 1, how the world works (the Big Cycle, its determinants, the cycles of money, credit and debt, internal and external order, investing); Part 2, the last 500 years (the Dutch, British, American and Chinese empires, and US–China relations); Part 3, the future.
Key terms: the Big Cycle · the eight determinants · reserve currency · the long-term debt cycle · hard, claims-on-hard and fiat money · the six stages of the internal cycle · the five kinds of war.
Key places and times: the Dutch Republic (1600s) · the British Empire (1800s) · Bretton Woods (1944) · the Nixon shock (1971) · the 1930–45 "destruction and rebuilding" period.

## The Changing World Order: what makes a country strong
- Dalio's eight measures of a country's power, the first four:
```parts
# any order
Taught well :: Education
Able to sell at good prices :: Competitiveness
New ideas :: Innovation and technology
How much it produces :: Economic output
```
- …and the other four:
```parts
# any order
Its part of world commerce :: Share of world trade
Armed forces :: Military strength
Where money is raised and traded :: Financial center
The currency others save in :: Reserve currency status
```
- Of the eight, the one that rises last and falls last is {{reserve currency status|which measure?}}: the world keeps saving in a currency long after the power behind it fades.

- A leading power loses its edge as its workers grow {{more expensive|what happens to its workers?}} and others copy its methods (British shipyards hired Dutch designers).

## The Changing World Order: money and debt
- The long-term debt cycle, as money changes:
```parts
Money is metal :: Hard money (gold, silver)
Paper you can trade for metal :: Claims on hard money
More promises than metal :: Debt grows, then crises
Paper backed by nothing :: Fiat money, printed and devalued
Savers lose faith :: The flight back to hard money
```
- On August 15, 1971, {{Richard Nixon|who?}} ended the dollar's link to gold, the end of the Bretton Woods system.
also in: Cold War (1945–1991)
- The Bretton Woods monetary system, agreed in {{1944|when?}} in New Hampshire, set the dollar at the center of the postwar order.
also in: Modern America and World Wars (1914–1945)
- The four ways to bring debts down:
```parts
# any order
Spend less :: Austerity
Don't pay in full :: Defaults and restructuring
Take from those who have more :: Transfers (higher taxes)
Make more money :: Printing money and devaluing it
```
- Of the four, the one governments reach for most, because it hurts least at first, is {{printing money|which?}}.
- Of about 750 currencies since 1700, only about {{20 percent|how many?}} still exist, and every one has been devalued.
- Dalio's warning about money: "{{Debt eats equity|what phrase?}}": the claims of lenders come before what owners keep.

## The Changing World Order: order within a country
- The six stages of the internal cycle, the first three:
```parts
New leaders take charge :: The new order consolidates power
Building the system :: Bureaucracies and resource allocation are built
The good years :: Peace and prosperity
```
- …and the last three:
```parts
Living too large :: Excess spending and debt, widening gaps
Money runs short, tempers run high :: Bad finances and intense conflict
The breaking point :: Civil war or revolution
```
- In the 1930s the same pressures brought a peaceful redistribution in the United States, {{Roosevelt's New Deal|what?}}, and violent revolutions elsewhere.
also in: Modern America and World Wars (1914–1945)

## The Changing World Order: order between countries
- Dalio's five kinds of war between countries:
```parts
# any order
Tariffs and sanctions on goods :: Trade and economic war
Who gets which chips and know-how :: Technology war
Territory and alliances :: Geopolitical war
Cutting off money and credit :: Capital war
Shooting :: Military war
```
- Military war is most likely when two powers are {{roughly equal in strength|how matched?}} and have irreconcilable, existential differences.
- Dalio's lesson from the Cold War: the financial strength to {{outspend|what?}} a rival can win without a shooting war.

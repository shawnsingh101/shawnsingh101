## A life in brief

**Václav Havel** (1936–2011) was born in **Prague** into a rich family of builders and businessmen: his grandfather
built the **Lucerna** palace and his uncle the **Barrandov** film studios. After the communist takeover of 1948 the
family lost everything, and as a "bourgeois" child Havel was barred from the schools he wanted. He worked as a lab
technician and took night classes.

### The theater
He found work as a stagehand at the **Theatre on the Balustrade**, a small Prague stage, and became its playwright.
His absurdist comedies about bureaucracy and empty language, ***The Garden Party*** (1963) and *The Memorandum* (1965),
were hits at home and abroad.

### The dissident
After the Soviet-led invasion ended the **Prague Spring** in 1968, his plays were banned. He worked in a **brewery**,
which became the setting of his play *Audience*. In 1977 he was one of the founders of **Charter 77**, a petition
demanding that the government keep its own human-rights promises. His essay ***The Power of the Powerless*** (1978)
told of a greengrocer who hangs "Workers of the world, unite!" in his window without believing it: the regime lives
on such small lies, and ends when people "live in truth." He spent nearly five years in prison, the longest stretch
1979–83, writing *Letters to Olga* to his wife, **Olga**.

### The Velvet Revolution
In November 1989 police beat student marchers in Prague; within days, hundreds of thousands filled **Wenceslas
Square**. Havel's **Civic Forum** led the peaceful **Velvet Revolution**, and on 29 December parliament elected him
**president**. Crowds had chanted "Havel na Hrad!" (Havel to the Castle!).

### President
He resigned in 1992 rather than preside over the split of Czechoslovakia, then served as the first president of the
**Czech Republic** (1993–2003), taking it toward NATO and the EU. He died at his country cottage, **Hrádeček**, in
2011. Prague's airport now bears his name.

The Budapest prodigy who founded game theory, put quantum mechanics on firm mathematical ground, helped design the atomic bomb, and drew up the plan every modern computer still follows.

:::lesson
## A life in brief

**John von Neumann** (1903–1957) was born **Neumann János** in **Budapest**, then in Austria-Hungary, into a wealthy
Jewish banking family; his father's title of nobility gave him the "von." As a child he could divide eight-digit numbers
in his head and joke in Ancient Greek. At the **Fasori Lutheran Gymnasium**, a year behind the future Nobel physicist
**Eugene Wigner**, his teacher saw his gift and found him university tutors.

### Two degrees at once
To please his father he took a degree in **chemical engineering** at ETH Zurich, while finishing a doctorate in
mathematics in Budapest, both in 1926. He then taught in Berlin, one of the youngest lecturers there.

### Princeton
In 1930 he went to **Princeton**, and in 1933 he was one of the first professors of the **Institute for Advanced
Study**, with Einstein. His 1932 book gave **quantum mechanics** its rigorous mathematics. With the economist **Oskar
Morgenstern** he wrote *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944), founding **game theory** on his minimax theorem.

### The bomb
At **Los Alamos** he worked out the **implosion** design: shaped explosives squeezing a plutonium core, the method of
the Trinity test and the Nagasaki bomb. He also helped choose the height at which bombs should burst.

### The computer
In 1945 his *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC* described a machine that stores its **program in the same memory as
its data**: the **von Neumann architecture**, still the plan of nearly every computer. He built one at the Institute,
the IAS machine, and with Stanisław Ulam developed the **Monte Carlo method**. He also ran the first computer weather
forecasts and imagined self-copying machines (cellular automata).

### The man
He loved loud parties, fast cars he often crashed, and sharp suits. He served on the Atomic Energy Commission,
and died of cancer in 1957, aged 53, at Walter Reed hospital, with a military guard at his door in case he spoke
secrets in his sleep.

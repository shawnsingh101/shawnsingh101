## A life in brief

**J. Robert Oppenheimer** (1904–1967) was born in **New York City** to a wealthy German-Jewish family; his father had
made a fortune importing textiles. He went to the **Ethical Culture** school, collected minerals, finished
**Harvard** in three years, and went to Europe for physics: an unhappy year at **Cambridge**, then a doctorate at
**Göttingen** under Max Born, where the two worked out the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation**.

### Berkeley
From 1929 he taught at **Berkeley** and Caltech and built the leading school of theoretical physics in America. In
1939, with Hartland Snyder, he showed how a dying star could collapse into what we now call a **black hole**. He loved
New Mexico, where he rented a ranch, read Sanskrit to read the **Bhagavad Gita**, and moved in left-wing circles. In
1940 he married **Kitty Puening**.

### Los Alamos
In 1942 General **Leslie Groves** chose him to lead the bomb laboratory of the **Manhattan Project**. Oppenheimer
picked the site, a boys' ranch school on a mesa at **Los Alamos**. On 16 July 1945 the **Trinity** test lit the New
Mexico desert; he later recalled a line from the Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." Weeks later
the bombs fell on **Hiroshima** and **Nagasaki**.

### After the war
He chaired the Atomic Energy Commission's advisers and opposed a crash program for the **hydrogen bomb**. In 1947 he
became director of the **Institute for Advanced Study** in Princeton. In **1954** a security hearing, pressed by AEC
chairman **Lewis Strauss**, revoked his clearance; **Edward Teller** testified against him. In 1963 President Johnson
gave him the **Enrico Fermi Award** as a gesture of repair. He died of throat cancer in 1967. In 2022 the government
vacated the 1954 decision.

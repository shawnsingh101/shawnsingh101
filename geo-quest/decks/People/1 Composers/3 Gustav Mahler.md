## A life in brief

**Gustav Mahler** (1860–1911) was born in **Kaliště**, a Bohemian village, to a German-speaking Jewish family, and
grew up in the town of **Jihlava**. He was one of fourteen children; many died young, and death runs through his music.
"I am thrice homeless," he said: a Bohemian among Austrians, an Austrian among Germans, a Jew throughout the world.

### The conductor
After the **Vienna Conservatory** he climbed through the opera houses of central Europe: Ljubljana, Olomouc, Kassel,
Prague, Leipzig, **Budapest** and **Hamburg**. In 1897 he converted to Catholicism, a condition for the top job in
Vienna, and became director of the **Vienna Court Opera**. In ten years he remade it, darkening the house, banning
latecomers, and staging operas as drama.

### The summer composer
Conducting filled the season, so Mahler composed in summer, in small huts built for him by a lake or in the woods:
at **Steinbach on the Attersee**, then **Maiernigg on the Wörthersee**, and finally **Toblach** in the Dolomites. "A
symphony must be like the world," he told Sibelius. "It must embrace everything."

### Alma
In 1902 he married **Alma Schindler**, nineteen years younger and herself a composer, whom he asked to give up
composing. 1907 was his worst year: their elder daughter **Maria** died of diphtheria, he learned he had a heart
condition, and he left the Vienna Opera after years of hostile, often antisemitic, press.

### New York and the end
He conducted at the **Metropolitan Opera** and then the **New York Philharmonic**. In 1910, troubled by Alma's affair
with the architect Walter Gropius, he walked for an afternoon in Leiden with **Sigmund Freud**. That autumn his
huge **Eighth Symphony** triumphed in Munich. He fell ill in New York, sailed home, and died in Vienna in May 1911,
aged 50, leaving the **Tenth** unfinished. "My time will come," he had said; from the 1960s, it did.

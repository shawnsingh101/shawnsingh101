## A life in brief

**Franz Kline** (1910–1962) was born in **Wilkes-Barre**, a coal and railroad city in northeastern Pennsylvania. His
father died when he was seven, and he was sent to **Girard College** in Philadelphia, a boarding school for fatherless
boys. He studied art at **Boston University** and at **Heatherley's** school in London, where he met his wife,
**Elizabeth Vincent Parsons**, a British ballet dancer.

### Struggling in New York
In 1938 he settled in **Greenwich Village**. For ten years he scraped by painting cityscapes, portraits, and murals for
bars, in a realist style.

### The projector
Around 1949, at **Willem de Kooning**'s studio, Kline put some of his small brush sketches into a projector. Blown up
on the wall, a few inches of ink became huge, abstract shapes. From then on he painted big: broad **black strokes on
white**, made with house painters' brushes and cheap enamel, like giant calligraphy or steel girders. His first solo
show, at the **Egan Gallery** in 1950, made his name as one of the **Abstract Expressionists** of the New York School,
beside Pollock and de Kooning.

### The titles
He often named paintings after places and trains in the Pennsylvania of his youth: *Chief* (1950, MoMA) for a train,
*Mahoning* (1956, Whitney Museum) for a place near his home.

### Later years
He taught at **Black Mountain College** in 1952, brought color back into his work in the late 1950s, and died of heart
failure in New York in 1962, aged 51.

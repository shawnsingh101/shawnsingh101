Putting numbers on risk, the four ways to deal with it, and the documents that track it.

:::lesson
## Measuring risk
**Qualitative** analysis rates risks low, medium or high. **Quantitative** analysis puts money on them:

$$ALE = SLE \times ARO$$

- **SLE** (single loss expectancy): what one occurrence costs: the asset's value × the **exposure factor** (the share lost).
- **ARO** (annualized rate of occurrence): how many times a year it's expected.
- **ALE** (annualized loss expectancy): the expected cost per year.

A $200,000 warehouse, half destroyed by a flood (exposure factor 50%), loses $100,000 each time; floods come once in 20 years (ARO 0.05), so the ALE is $5,000 a year. Spending more than that a year on flood protection doesn't pay.

## Four ways to respond
- **Avoid**: stop the risky activity altogether.
- **Mitigate** (reduce): add controls.
- **Transfer**: shift the cost to someone else, most often by **insurance**.
- **Accept**: live with it, usually because fixing it costs more; sometimes by a formal **exception**.

Whatever is left after controls is **residual risk**. How much risk the organization is willing to carry is its **risk appetite**; its **risk tolerance** is how far from that it can stray.

## Tracking it
A **risk register** lists each risk, its owner, its rating and the response. Every risk has an **owner**, accountable for it.

## Third parties
Suppliers bring risk in: **vendor assessments**, the right to audit, and agreements: an **SLA** (service-level agreement) sets performance; an **MOU** (memorandum of understanding) is a less formal statement of intent; an **NDA** keeps secrets secret.
:::

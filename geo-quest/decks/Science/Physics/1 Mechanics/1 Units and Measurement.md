## 1.1 Scope and scale
related: Physics, scientific model, order of magnitude
- A scientific {{model|what kind of tool?}} keeps the features needed to answer a question and leaves out details that do not affect that answer.
- A model is useful only within its {{domain of validity|what limits where it works?}}.
- An order-of-magnitude estimate keeps the nearest power of {{ten|what base?}} and ignores detail smaller than that scale.
- If a model predicts a result far outside the measured range, its {{assumptions|what should be reexamined?}} need testing before trusting the prediction.
- In a physical measurement, a number without a {{unit|what is missing?}} does not fully state the quantity.

## 1.2 Standards
related: International System of Units, SI base unit, physical quantity
![Seven SI base-unit symbols · Randomwangran · CC BY-SA 4.0](commons:SI base units .png)
- The SI base unit for length is the {{metre (m)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for mass is the {{kilogram (kg)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for time is the {{second (s)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for electric current is the {{ampere (A)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for thermodynamic temperature is the {{kelvin (K)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for amount of substance is the {{mole (mol)|which unit?}}.
- The SI base unit for luminous intensity is the {{candela (cd)|which unit?}}.
- A physical quantity is written as a numerical value multiplied by a {{unit|what accompanies the number?}}.
- A unit built from base units is a {{derived unit|what kind of unit?}}; for example, $1\ \mathrm{m/s}=1\ \mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.
- The SI prefix kilo- means a factor of ${{10^3}}$; milli- means ${{10^{-3}}}$.
- The SI prefix micro- means a factor of ${{10^{-6}}}$; nano- means ${{10^{-9}}}$.
- A measurement standard lets different observers realize the same {{unit|what is being made reproducible?}}.
- The modern SI defines its base units through fixed values of {{physical constants|what quantities?}}.

## 1.3 Rewriting measurements
related: Unit of measurement, dimensional analysis
- A conversion factor is a ratio equal to {{one|what number?}} because its numerator and denominator describe the same quantity.
- To convert $72\ \mathrm{km/h}$ to metres per second, multiply by $\frac{1000\ \mathrm{m}}{1\ \mathrm{km}}\frac{1\ \mathrm{h}}{3600\ \mathrm{s}}$; the result is ${{20\ \mathrm{m/s}}}$.
- In a correct unit conversion, unwanted units {{cancel|what happens to them?}} like algebraic factors.
- Convert $250\ \mathrm{cm}$ to metres: $250\ \mathrm{cm}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{m}}{100\ \mathrm{cm}}={{2.5\ \mathrm{m}}}$.
- A conversion factor changes the {{unit|what changes?}} while preserving the physical quantity.

## 1.4 Dimensional analysis
related: Dimensional analysis, physical quantity
- The dimensions of position are length, written ${{[x]=L}}$.
- The dimensions of speed are ${{[v]=LT^{-1}}}$.
- The dimensions of acceleration are ${{[a]=LT^{-2}}}$.
- The dimensions of force are ${{[F]=MLT^{-2}}}$.
- Terms added in a physical equation must have the same {{dimensions|what must match?}}.
- Dimensional consistency can reject an equation, but it cannot determine a dimensionless {{constant|what can it not reveal?}}.
- If $x=vt$ describes distance from constant speed, its dimensions match because $[vt]=LT^{-1}\,T={{L}}$.
- A proposed period $T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ has dimensions of {{time|what dimension?}}, since $[L/g]=T^2$.

## 1.5 Estimates and Fermi calculations
related: Fermi problem, estimation
- A Fermi estimate replaces hard-to-measure details with a small set of {{reasonable assumptions|what supplies the estimate?}}.
- A useful estimate is built by breaking the target into simpler {{factors|what gets multiplied or counted?}}.
- Estimate the number of breaths in a day using $16$ breaths/min and $1440$ min/day: $16\times1440\approx {{2.3\times10^4}}$ breaths.
- A Fermi estimate is most useful when its assumptions and approximate {{scale|what should the answer establish?}} are visible.

## 1.6 Significant figures
related: Significant figures, measurement uncertainty
- Significant figures communicate the {{precision|what aspect of a measurement?}} supported by the reported value.
- In $0.00470$, the significant digits are {{4, 7, and the final 0|which digits?}}; leading zeros only locate the decimal point.
- In scientific notation, the number of significant figures is clear from the digits in the {{coefficient|which part?}}.
- For multiplication and division, a result is usually rounded to the same number of significant figures as the least precise {{input|which value?}}.
- For addition and subtraction, round to the least precise {{decimal place|what place?}} among the inputs.
- Exact counted quantities and defined unit conversions do not limit the significant figures of a {{calculation|what result?}}.
- A measured value should be understood together with its {{uncertainty|what accompanies it?}}.

## 1.7 Solving problems in physics
related: Scientific method, mathematical model
- Start a quantitative physics problem by identifying the system, known quantities, target quantity, and relevant {{model|what frames the calculation?}}.
- A diagram can expose geometry, directions, and relationships before the {{equations|what gets chosen next?}}.
- Check a calculated answer with units, limiting cases, and an independent estimate of its {{scale|what should be plausible?}}.
- A numerical answer that violates a known physical constraint is evidence to revisit the {{setup|what part of the solution?}}.

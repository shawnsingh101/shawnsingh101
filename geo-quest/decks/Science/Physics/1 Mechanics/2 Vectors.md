## 2.1 Choosing representations
related: Vector, scalar (physics), physical quantity
- A scalar is specified by magnitude; a vector requires magnitude and {{direction|what else?}}.
- Mass and temperature are {{scalars|what kind of quantities?}}; displacement and force are vectors.
- Two vectors are equal when their magnitudes and directions match, even if their drawn arrows start at different {{points|what locations?}}.
- A vector's magnitude is a nonnegative {{scalar|what kind of quantity?}}.
- A vector can be represented by an arrow whose length gives its magnitude and whose orientation gives its {{direction|what does the arrow show?}}.
- A vector quantity cannot be added meaningfully to a quantity with {{different dimensions|what prevents the operation?}}.

## 2.2 Coordinate systems and components
related: Coordinate system, Cartesian coordinate system, unit vector
![Cartesian vector components](commons:Cartesian vector components.svg)
- A coordinate system specifies an origin, axes, and a rule for assigning {{coordinates|what describes a point?}}.
- In a right-handed Cartesian system, the positive $z$ direction follows $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}}$.
- In two dimensions, a vector with magnitude $A$ at angle $\theta$ from +x has $A_x={{A\cos\theta}}$ and $A_y={{A\sin\theta}}$.
eq: Vector components
- For $\mathbf{A}=3\hat{\mathbf{i}}+4\hat{\mathbf{j}}$, the magnitude is $|\mathbf{A}|={{5}}$.
eq: Vector magnitude
- Unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{i}},\hat{\mathbf{j}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ point along the positive {{x, y, and z axes|which directions?}} and have magnitude one.
- The angle used in $A_x=A\cos\theta$ is measured from the {{positive x-axis|which reference direction?}}.
- A vector in three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates is specified by its {{x, y, and z components|which numbers?}}.

## 2.3 Algebra of vectors
related: Vector, vector addition, linear combination
- Add vectors by adding corresponding {{components|what values are combined?}}.
- Vector addition is {{commutative|what property lets the order switch?}}: $\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}$.
- Subtracting $\mathbf{B}$ means adding its {{opposite|what vector?}}: $\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}+(-\mathbf{B})$.
- Multiplying a vector by a positive scalar changes its magnitude but keeps its {{direction|what stays fixed?}}.
- Multiplying a vector by a negative scalar reverses its direction and scales its {{magnitude|what else changes?}}.
- A vector whose magnitude is 0 has no defined {{direction|what property is undefined?}}.
- If $\mathbf{A}=2\hat{\mathbf{i}}-\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ and $\mathbf{B}=\hat{\mathbf{i}}+3\hat{\mathbf{j}}$, then $\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}={{3\hat{\mathbf{i}}+2\hat{\mathbf{j}}}}$.

## 2.4 Products
related: Dot product, cross product, right-hand rule
- The dot product of two vectors is a {{scalar|what type of result?}}.
- $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}=AB\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors.
eq: Dot product
- Perpendicular vectors have dot product {{zero|what value?}} because $\cos 90^\circ=0$.
- When the angle between two vectors is obtuse, their dot product is {{negative|what sign?}}.
- The cross product is a {{vector|what type of result?}} perpendicular to the plane containing the two input vectors.
- The magnitude of $\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}$ is ${{AB\sin\theta}}$.
eq: Cross product magnitude
- The cross-product direction follows the {{right-hand rule|which rule?}}.
- Parallel vectors have cross product {{zero|what value?}} because the angle between them is $0$ or $180^\circ$.
- Reversing the order of a cross product reverses its direction: $\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}=-({{\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{A}}})$.
- The dot product measures how much one vector points along the other; the cross product's magnitude measures the {{parallelogram area|what geometric quantity?}} spanned by them.

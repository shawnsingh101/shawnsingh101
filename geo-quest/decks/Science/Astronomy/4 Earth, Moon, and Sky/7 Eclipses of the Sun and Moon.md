## A coincidence of size
The Sun is about **400 times wider** than the Moon and also about **400 times farther away**, so both look about **half a degree** across. That is why the Moon can just cover the Sun.

## Shadows
Every shadow has two parts: the dark inner cone, the **umbra**, where the light is fully blocked, and the paler outer **penumbra**, where it is only partly blocked.

- A **solar eclipse** is the Moon's shadow falling on Earth. It can only happen at **new moon**.
- A **lunar eclipse** is the Moon passing into Earth's shadow. It can only happen at **full moon**.

They don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about **5°** to Earth's orbit (the ecliptic): most months the Moon passes above or below the Sun. Eclipses come only in the **eclipse seasons**, about twice a year, when the Moon crosses the ecliptic near new or full moon.

## Solar eclipses
```stack
# Three kinds of solar eclipse
Total | in the umbra | the Moon covers the whole Sun; the corona appears
Annular | beyond the umbra's tip | the Moon is a bit too far to cover it: a "ring of fire"
Partial | in the penumbra | a bite out of the Sun; seen over a wide area
```
The Moon's orbit is an ellipse; when it's farther than average it looks smaller than the Sun, and a central eclipse is **annular** (from Latin *anulus*, ring). The umbra's tip races across Earth at around 1,500 km/h, drawing a narrow **path of totality**; totality never lasts more than about **7½ minutes**. During it the sky darkens, stars and planets come out, animals go quiet, and the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the **corona**, appears. The last two total eclipses crossing the US were **August 21, 2017** and **April 8, 2024**; the next to cross the contiguous US is in **2044**.

**Safety**: never look at a partly covered Sun without certified eclipse glasses or a welder's #14 filter (sunglasses and smoked glass are not safe); a **pinhole projector**, or the gaps between leaves, casts safe crescent images. Only during totality is it safe to look with the naked eye.

## Lunar eclipses
Earth's umbra is large, several Moons wide at the Moon's distance, so a total lunar eclipse can last up to about **1 hour 40 minutes**, and it is visible from the whole **night side** of Earth at once. That is why most people see more lunar than solar eclipses, though solar eclipses are actually a little more frequent worldwide. The eclipsed Moon turns **coppery red**: sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere reaches it, with the blue scattered out, as in a sunset. A lunar eclipse is safe to watch with the naked eye.

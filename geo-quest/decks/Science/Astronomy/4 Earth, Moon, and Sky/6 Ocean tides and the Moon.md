## A difference in pull
The Moon pulls on all of Earth, but not equally: the near side is pulled **harder** than Earth's center, and the center harder than the far side. That **difference** in pull, the **tidal force**, stretches Earth slightly along the Earth–Moon line. It's too small to move rocks or people, but it nudges the oceans sideways, and over hours the water flows into **two bulges**: one under the Moon, one on the opposite side. (The water isn't lifted "up"; it piles up by flowing in from the sides.)

As Earth turns under the two bulges, most coasts get **two high tides and two low tides a day**, each a little later than the day before, about **50 minutes**, keeping step with the Moon. Even the solid Earth bulges, by up to about 20 cm.

## The Sun's share
The Sun raises tides too, a bit **under half** as strong as the Moon's (it is far heavier, but much farther away, and tidal force falls off with the **cube** of distance).

```stack
# Two kinds of tide, twice a month
Spring tides | Sun, Moon and Earth in a line | new and full moon: the two pulls add; highest highs, lowest lows
Neap tides | Sun and Moon at right angles | first and third quarter: the pulls partly cancel; the smallest range
```
"Spring" has nothing to do with the season: the tide *springs up*.

## The real ocean
Coastlines, the depth of the seafloor, friction and Earth's spin make real tides far more complicated than two neat bulges, which is why every port has its own **tide table**. Some places, like parts of the Mediterranean, barely have tides; the **Bay of Fundy** in Canada has the world's largest, up to about **16 meters**.

## The Moon is leaving
Tidal friction is a brake on Earth's spin: the day grows by about **2 milliseconds per century**. The spin Earth loses goes into the Moon's orbit, which grows: laser reflectors left by **Apollo 11** show the Moon moving away about **3.8 cm a year**. **George Darwin**, Charles Darwin's son, worked out this give and take in the 1880s. In billions of years the day and the month would match, and total solar eclipses will have ended long before, as the Moon becomes too small in our sky to cover the Sun.

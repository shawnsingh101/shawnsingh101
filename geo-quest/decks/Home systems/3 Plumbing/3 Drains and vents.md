How used water leaves: drains that flow by gravity, the trap under every fixture, the vents that let it all flow, the main stack, and the sewer or septic system at the end.

## How drains work
- Drain pipes carry water out by {{gravity|what force?}}, not pressure.
- A drain line slopes about {{¼ inch per foot|how much?}} toward the sewer.
- The plumbing that carries used water out and lets air in is the {{drain-waste-vent|which system?}} system (DWV).
- Most drain pipe in houses today is black {{ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene)|what plastic?}} or white PVC; older houses have cast iron.

## Traps and vents
- A {{P-trap|which part?}} holds a little water in its curve, which blocks sewer gas from coming up the drain.
- A trap that sits unused until its water evaporates lets in {{sewer gas|what?}}.
- {{Vent pipes|which pipes?}} let air into the drains so water flows freely and doesn't siphon the traps dry; they come out through the roof.
![A plumbing vent on a roof](commons:SoilStack.PNG)
- The {{main stack|which pipe?}} is the large vertical pipe that the toilets drain into; it continues up through the roof as a vent.
- A capped opening for snaking out a blocked drain is a {{cleanout|what?}}.

## Where it goes
- The house drain leaves through the {{sewer lateral|which pipe?}} to the city sewer, or to a septic tank.
- In a {{septic tank|what?}}, solids settle to the bottom as sludge and grease floats on top as scum.
![A septic tank](commons:Septic tank Bolduc.jpg)
- From a septic tank, the liquid flows out to the {{drain field|what?}} (leach field), where the soil filters it.
- A household septic tank should be inspected, and usually pumped, every {{3 to 5 years|how often?}}.

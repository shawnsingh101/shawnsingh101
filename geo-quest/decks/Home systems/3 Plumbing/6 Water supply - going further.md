## Trunks and branches
Supply pipes are laid out like a tree. **Trunk lines**, usually ¾ inch, carry hot and cold water along the basement to the areas that need it; **branch lines**, usually ½ inch, run to one or two fixtures; a toilet's own supply tube is often ⅜ inch. Every fixture should have its own shutoff valves, so fixing one sink doesn't mean shutting off the house.

## Lifting water from a well
Air pressure can only push water up a pipe about 33 feet, and in practice a pump that **sucks** from above can lift it about 25. That sets the three kinds of well pumps apart:
- A **hand (pitcher) pump** lifts water with a leather-sealed plunger and two flap valves. Dry leather leaks, so a pump left unused has to be **primed**: pour water in the top.
- A **jet pump** sits beside the house and still works by suction, helped by the **Venturi effect**: some of the pumped water is sent back down through a narrow nozzle; water that speeds up drops in pressure, so the jet pulls more water along with it. A **foot valve** at the bottom of the pipe stops the water from draining back into the well.
- A **submersible pump** sits underwater at the bottom of the well and **pushes** instead of sucking, so it can lift water from hundreds of feet down and never needs priming. It is a stack of **stages**, each a spinning impeller that adds pressure. The pipe leaves the well casing below the frost line through a **pitless adapter**.

## The pressure tank
![A bladder pressure tank, nearly empty and full (drawn for this app)](figures/home/pressure-tank.svg)
Without a tank, the pump would start every time someone filled a glass. The tank holds a cushion of compressed air: water pumped in squeezes the air, and the air then pushes the water out to the taps. The **pressure switch** starts the pump when the pressure falls to its low setting and stops it at the high one (often 40 and 60 psi). In old tanks the water slowly absorbed the air until the tank was **waterlogged** and the pump **short-cycled**, starting every few seconds. Modern tanks keep the air in its own space behind a rubber **bladder**, charged through a tire-style valve to about 2 psi below the pump's turn-on pressure.

## Below the sewer
House drains usually leave above the basement floor, so a basement toilet can't drain by gravity. It drains instead into a sealed pit, where a **sewage ejector pump** (or a grinder pump) switched by a **float** lifts the waste up into the house drain, past a **check valve** that keeps it from falling back.

## Fire sprinklers
Heat from a fire rises to the ceiling. When the air at a sprinkler head reaches its rating (often about 155 °F), a liquid-filled glass bulb bursts or a metal link melts, and that one head opens. The spray cools the fire mostly by turning to steam, which soaks up a great deal of heat. Only the heads near the fire open.

## The pressure tank
![A bladder pressure tank (drawn for this app)](figures/home/pressure-tank.svg)
- A modern pressure tank keeps its air apart from the water with a rubber {{bladder|what?}} (or diaphragm).
- An old tank without a bladder slowly loses its air into the water and becomes {{waterlogged|what?}}.
- A pump that switches on and off every few seconds is {{short-cycling|what?}}, usually because the tank has lost its air cushion.
- With the tank empty, a bladder tank's air charge is set about {{2 psi|how much?}} below the pump's turn-on pressure.

## Below the sewer
- A basement bathroom below the sewer line drains into a sealed pit, where a {{sewage ejector pump|which pump?}} lifts the waste up to the house drain.
- A {{float switch|what?}} turns a sump or ejector pump on when the level in its pit rises.
- A {{check valve|which valve?}} in a pump's discharge pipe keeps the pumped water from falling back into the pit.

The Plumbing deep dive, part 4: the simple repairs that fix most toilet, faucet and drain problems before you call a plumber, low-flow and composting toilets, and what each faucet type needs when it drips. Order follows chapter 1 of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); the cards are our own.

:::lesson
## Before calling a plumber
Most fixture "repairs" are a worn rubber part, a loose nut or a clog. Shut off the fixture's own valves first, put a towel in the sink so small parts can't fall down the drain, and take the old part to the store to match it.

| Problem | Most likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Toilet runs or refills by itself | leaky flapper | new flapper |
| Water runs into the overflow tube | float set too high | lower the float |
| Toilet leaks at the base | old wax ring | pull the toilet, new wax ring |
| Compression faucet drips | worn washer | new washer and screw |
| Ball faucet drips | worn seats and springs | new seats and springs |
| Cartridge faucet drips | worn O-rings or cartridge | new O-rings, else a new cartridge |
| Disk faucet drips | worn seals under the cylinder | new seals |
| Leak under a single-handle spout | worn body O-rings | new O-rings, greased |
| Sink drains slowly | hair on the stopper | pull the stopper and clean it |

## Toilets that use less
Since 1994 a new US toilet may use 1.6 gallons a flush; WaterSense models use 1.28 or less, and **dual-flush** toilets give a half flush for liquids. A **composting toilet** uses no water at all: it keeps urine apart from solids, which then compost into a dry, earthy material.
:::

## Toilet repairs
- A toilet that leaks water around its base needs a new {{wax ring|what?}}.
- A toilet that refills by itself every so often (a "phantom flush") has a leaking {{flapper|which part?}}.
- To test for a flapper leak, put a few drops of {{food coloring|what?}} in the tank and see whether color reaches the bowl without a flush.
- If water keeps running into the overflow tube, lower the {{float|which part?}} so the fill valve shuts sooner.
- A broken toilet lift chain can be fixed for now with a plastic {{zip tie|what?}}.
- A {{dual-flush|which kind?}} toilet has two buttons: a small flush for liquids and a full one for solids.

## Faucet repairs
- In a ball faucet, two rubber {{seats|what?}} on small springs press against the ball; when they wear, the spout drips.
- When new seats and springs don't stop a single-handle faucet's drip, replace the {{ball|what part?}} they press on, ideally with a stainless steel one.
- When new O-rings don't stop the drip, replace the {{cartridge|which part?}}, the tube with holes that the handle moves; take the old one along to match it.
- A faucet cartridge is held in the faucet body by a {{retaining clip|what?}}.
- When a disk faucet drips, the disks are rarely to blame: replace the rubber {{seals|what?}} under the cylinder.
- After repairing a disk faucet, open the handle {{before|before or after?}} turning the water back on, so the rush of air can't crack the disks.
- A leak from under the spout of a single-handle faucet is fixed with new {{O-rings|what?}} on the faucet body.
- A shower that runs cooler than its control's setting may just need the {{water heater|what?}} turned up.

## Drain repairs
- A slow bathroom sink is most often clogged with {{hair|what?}} caught on the stopper.
- A sink stopper that won't stay up or down needs the {{retaining nut|which part?}} on the pivot rod tightened.
- To raise or lower a sink stopper, move the pivot rod to a different hole in the {{clevis|which part?}}.
- A plunger-type tub drain that won't hold water needs its plunger's linkage made {{longer or shorter|what change?}} to seat the plug.
- To clear a clogged tub drain, take off the overflow plate and feed a {{drain auger|what tool?}} down the overflow pipe.

## Outdoor faucet repairs
- A sillcock that drips when it's shut needs a new {{washer|what part?}}.
- A sillcock that leaks from under its handle when open needs its {{packing nut|which part?}} tightened, or new packing.

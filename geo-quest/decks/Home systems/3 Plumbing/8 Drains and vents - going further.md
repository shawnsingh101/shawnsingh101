The Plumbing deep dive, part 3: the names of every part of the drain system, why traps need vents, the code's ways of venting a hard-to-reach fixture, and how a septic system works and fails. Order follows chapter 1 of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); the cards and drawings are our own.

:::lesson
## The waste system
Water leaves each fixture through its **trap**, then a short **trap arm** to the point where air comes in from a vent. Small **branch** drains run into a big vertical **stack**; the part that carries toilet waste is the **soil stack**, at least 3 inches across. The stack drops to the **house drain**, the lowest horizontal pipe, which becomes the **house sewer** (at least 4 inches) outside the wall. Horizontal drains slope ⅛ to ¼ inch per foot: too flat and solids settle out, too steep and the water runs ahead and leaves them behind. Y-shaped **cleanouts** give a way in for a drain snake: one where the drain leaves the house, and more along long runs and sharp turns.

## Why traps need vents
![A trap, its arm and its vent (drawn for this app)](figures/home/trap-and-vent.svg)
A trap's few inches of water are all that keep sewer gas out of the house. When a sink empties, the slug of water running down the drain can pull the air behind it, and with it the water in the trap: the trap is **siphoned** dry. A **vent** close behind the trap lets outdoor air into the pipe, so the pressure stays even and the seal stays. That's why the **trap arm** has a maximum length (it depends on the pipe size), and why the old **S-trap**, which drops straight down, is no longer allowed. A toilet's built-in trap is S-shaped, but its refill tube tops it back up after every flush.

## Ways to vent
- **Vent stack**: the stack above the highest drain, open through the roof.
- **Revent**: a separate vent pipe rising from a drain and joining the vent stack at least 6 inches above the highest fixture's flood rim, so water can never fill it.
- **Wet vent**: a pipe that is a drain for one fixture and the vent for another, allowed when it's big enough.
- **Loop vent**: for an island sink with no wall to run a vent up.
- **Air admittance valve**: a one-way valve that lets air in and keeps gas from coming out, for a single fixture where a regular vent is impractical.

## The septic system
![A septic system (drawn for this app)](figures/home/septic.svg)
Waste flows into the **septic tank**, where heavy solids sink as **sludge** and grease floats as **scum**; bacteria slowly break the solids down. The clearer liquid in the middle, the **effluent**, flows out past a baffle to a **distribution box**, which splits it among perforated pipes in gravel trenches: the **drain field**. There the soil filters and cleans it. If the field is uphill, a **pump chamber** with a float switch lifts the effluent to it and sounds an alarm if it backs up. If sludge builds up too far, solids reach the drain field and clog it, and a clogged field usually has to be replaced, at great cost. Regular pumping is what prevents that.

## Vents
- Below the highest drain connected to it, a stack is a waste stack; above, it's the {{vent stack|what?}}.
- A vent pipe that rises from a drain and joins the vent stack at least 6 inches above the highest fixture's rim is a {{revent|what?}}.
- A pipe that is a drain for one fixture and a vent for another is a {{wet vent|what?}}.
- An island sink, with no wall to run a vent up, can use a {{loop vent|what kind?}}.
- An {{air admittance valve|what?}} lets air into a drain but closes to keep sewer gas in; codes allow it where a regular vent is impractical.
- In cold climates, a roof vent can close up with {{frost|what?}} from the damp air inside it, so codes may call for a wider pipe above the roof.

Making the water better once it's in the house: softeners for hard water, carbon filters for taste and chlorine, reverse osmosis for dissolved salts and metals, and ultraviolet light for germs. Order follows chapter 1 of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); the cards are our own.

:::lesson
## Which treatment does what
| Treatment | Takes out | Upkeep |
|---|---|---|
| Water softener | calcium and magnesium (hardness) | add salt to the brine tank |
| Sediment filter | sand, rust, grit | change the cartridge when the flow slows |
| Activated carbon | chlorine, tastes, odors, many chemicals | change when the taste comes back |
| Reverse osmosis | most dissolved salts, metals, nitrates | change prefilters yearly, membrane every few years |
| UV light | bacteria, viruses, cysts (kills them) | change the lamp every year |

No one treatment does everything. A city supply is already disinfected, so city houses mostly use softeners and carbon. A well may need a test first: bacteria call for UV (after a sediment filter), and metals or nitrates for reverse osmosis.

## How a softener works
Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. They leave white scale in pipes, kettles and water heaters, and stop soap from lathering. A softener's tank is full of tiny plastic **resin beads** coated with sodium. As water passes, the beads grab the calcium and magnesium and let go of sodium in their place (**ion exchange**). When the beads are full, the softener **regenerates**, usually at night: it pulls salty water from the **brine tank**, which reloads the beads with sodium and flushes the hardness down the drain.

## Reverse osmosis, in stages
```stack
# An under-sink reverse-osmosis system, in order
5 | Carbon polishing filter | a last taste fix on the way to the tap
4 | Storage tank | RO is slow, so a small tank holds treated water
3 | RO membrane | lets water through, holds back dissolved salts; the reject goes down the drain
2 | Carbon prefilter | removes chlorine, which would damage the membrane
1 | Sediment filter | catches sand and rust
```

## Softening
- Water with a lot of dissolved calcium and magnesium is {{hard|what kind of water?}} water: it leaves scale and keeps soap from lathering.
- A {{water softener|what?}} swaps the calcium and magnesium in hard water for sodium (or potassium).
- Inside a softener, tiny plastic {{resin|what?}} beads catch the hardness minerals and give up sodium in their place.
- When its beads are full, a softener {{regenerates|what does it do?}}: it rinses them with salty brine and flushes the minerals down the drain.
- You add bags of salt to a softener's {{brine tank|what?}}.
- If softened water slowly turns hard again, first check whether the brine tank has run out of {{salt|what?}}.

## Filters
- An {{activated carbon|what material?}} filter removes chlorine, bad tastes and odors, which stick to its huge inner surface.
![Activated carbon](commons:Activated Carbon.jpg)
- A filter cartridge that slows the tap to a trickle is clogged with {{sediment|what?}} and needs replacing.
- A filter on the main line that treats all the water the house uses is a {{point-of-entry|which kind?}} filter; one under a single sink is point-of-use.
- {{Reverse osmosis|which treatment?}} pushes water through a membrane so fine that most dissolved salts, metals and chemicals stay behind.
- A reverse-osmosis system sends part of the water it treats down the {{drain|where?}}, carrying away what the membrane held back.

```parts
holds back dissolved salts :: RO membrane

```

## Ultraviolet light
- An {{ultraviolet (UV)|which kind?}} purifier kills bacteria and viruses with light, but removes no chemicals.
- A UV lamp should be replaced about once a {{year|how often?}}, since you usually can't see when it has weakened.
- A sediment filter goes {{before|before or after?}} a UV purifier, because particles can shade germs from the light.

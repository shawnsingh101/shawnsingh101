The Electrical deep dive, part 3: outlets and switches. Why plugs fit only one way, how a GFCI and an AFCI know to trip and where the code requires them, and how single-pole, three-way and four-way switches, dimmers and motion sensors work. Lights and alarms come next.

## How a GFCI knows
All the current that goes out on the hot wire should come back on the neutral. A GFCI runs both wires through a small magnetic ring; when the two currents are equal their fields cancel. If some current leaks out another way, through water or a person, the currents differ, the ring senses it, and the GFCI trips in about a fortieth of a second. It trips at a leak of about **5 milliamps**, well below what stops a heart.

## How an AFCI knows
An arc (current jumping a gap in a damaged, loose or chewed wire) is hot enough to start a fire inside a wall but may draw too little current to trip an ordinary breaker. An AFCI breaker has a small computer that watches the shape of the current and voltage for the ragged pattern an arc makes, and trips when it sees it.

## Three-way switches
A three-way switch has a **common** terminal (the dark screw) and two **traveler** terminals (light screws). The two switches are joined by two travelers. Flipping either switch moves its common from one traveler to the other, so the light can always be turned on or off from either end. A **four-way** switch sits between them and swaps the two travelers.

## Plugs and outlets
- On a standard plug, the {{neutral|which?}} blade is the wider one, so the plug goes in only one way.
- A standard 120-volt outlet is rated for 15 amps; a 20-amp outlet has a {{T-shaped|what shape?}} neutral slot that also takes 20-amp plugs.
- A plug's round third prong connects the appliance's metal case to the {{ground|which wire?}}.
- A three-prong plug forced into a two-slot outlet with a "cheater" adapter loses its {{ground|what?}} unless the adapter's tab is screwed to a grounded box.
- In a light socket, the hot wire must go to the {{center contact|which part?}} (brass screw), so the threaded shell is the neutral.

## How a GFCI knows
- A GFCI compares the current going out on the hot wire with the current coming back on the {{neutral|which wire?}}.
- A GFCI trips when as little as about {{5 milliamps|how much?}} leaks out of the circuit.
- A GFCI outlet's LOAD terminals protect every outlet {{downstream|where?}} of it on the same circuit.
- A GFCI should be tested every month with its {{TEST|which button?}} button, then reset.

## How an AFCI knows
- An {{arc|what?}} is current jumping a small gap, as in a frayed, loose or nail-pierced wire: hot enough to start a fire inside a wall.
- An AFCI breaker uses a tiny {{computer|what?}} to watch the current's pattern for the ragged signature of an arc.
- An AFCI breaker also has an ordinary thermal and {{magnetic|which?}} trip, so it does a regular breaker's job too.
- A breaker that combines AFCI and GFCI protection is a {{dual-function|what kind?}} breaker.

## Where the code requires them
- The National Electrical Code first required GFCI protection around {{swimming pools|where?}}, in the late 1960s and early 1970s.
- After pools and outdoor outlets, the code added GFCIs in {{bathrooms|where?}} in 1975, garages in 1978 and kitchen counters in 1987.
- AFCIs were first required in {{bedrooms|where?}}, in the 1999 code.
- An older house doesn't have to be rewired to the newest code: existing wiring is {{grandfathered|what?}} until it is remodeled.

## Switches
- A switch may only break the {{hot|which wire?}} wire, never the neutral.
- A switch that breaks both hot wires of a 240-volt circuit is a {{double-pole|which kind?}} switch.
- On a three-way switch, the dark screw is the {{common|which terminal?}} terminal.
- The two wires that run between a pair of three-way switches are the {{travelers|what?}}.
- A {{four-way|which kind?}} switch always sits between two three-way switches.
- A dimmer doesn't lower the voltage to the bulb; it switches it on and off {{120 times|how often?}} a second, for a smaller share of each cycle.
- An old-style dimmer may flicker or buzz with LED bulbs; use one marked {{LED-compatible|what kind?}}.
- Most motion-sensing light switches are {{passive infrared|which kind?}} (PIR) sensors, which see the heat of a moving body.
![A motion-sensing switch](commons:Light switch with passive infrared sensor.jpg)
- A motion sensor that turns lights on when you enter and off when you leave is an occupancy sensor; one you must switch on yourself, that only turns lights off, is a {{vacancy sensor|what?}}.

## Ceiling fixtures and lamps
Every ceiling fixture hangs from an **electrical box** fastened to the framing; a heavy chandelier or a fan needs a box rated to hold it. The wiring rule is the same as for any socket: the **hot** (black) wire goes to the brass screw and on to the small **center contact** at the bottom of the socket, and the **neutral** (white) goes to the silver screw and the threaded **shell**, the part a finger might touch while changing a bulb. Lamp cord isn't color-coded, so trace it: the wider plug blade is the neutral, and its conductor has ribs or a stripe on the insulation. In a table lamp, the two conductors are tied in an **underwriter's knot** inside the socket cap so a tug on the cord pulls on the knot, not on the screws.

## How a fluorescent tube makes light
A fluorescent tube holds a little **mercury vapor**. Current arcing through the vapor gives off ultraviolet light, which we can't see; a **phosphor** coating on the inside of the glass turns it into visible light. Once the arc starts, its resistance falls, so the current would run away without the **ballast**, which limits it. Older fixtures have a small plug-in **starter** that heats the filaments at each end for a moment before the arc strikes; modern electronic ballasts do this themselves. A compact fluorescent bulb (CFL) is the same thing folded up, with the ballast in its base.

## How an LED makes light
A **light-emitting diode** is a tiny chip of semiconductor that lets current flow only one way. As electrons cross the junction inside it they drop to a lower energy and give off the difference as light, of one color set by the material. Most **white** LEDs are blue ones coated with a yellow **phosphor**; blue plus yellow looks white. An LED bulb also holds a **driver**, a small circuit that turns the house's 120-volt alternating current into the low-voltage direct current the chips need, and a **heat sink**, because heat, not hours of use, is what wears an LED out.

## Measuring light
Watts measure the electricity a bulb uses; **lumens** measure the light it gives. An old 60-watt incandescent gave about 800 lumens; an LED gives the same from about 9 watts, and lasts about 15 to 25 times longer. The **color temperature**, in kelvins, says how warm or cool white light looks: about 2700 K is the yellowish white of an incandescent, 4000 K a neutral white, 5000 K and up a bluish daylight.

## Smoke alarms
An **ionization** alarm holds a speck of americium that makes the air in a small chamber conduct a tiny current; smoke particles drifting in disrupt it and set off the horn. It reacts fastest to flaming fires. A **photoelectric** alarm shines a light across a dark chamber, away from a sensor; smoke scatters some of the light onto the sensor. It reacts fastest to smoldering fires and gives fewer false alarms from cooking, so it's the kind to put near a kitchen. Since neither is best at everything, some alarms combine both.

![Hardwired, interconnected smoke alarms (drawn for this app)](figures/home/smoke-alarms.svg)
New houses must have alarms that are **hardwired** with a **battery backup** and **interconnected**, so one sensing smoke sets them all off. The first alarm gets power from a two-wire cable; a three-wire cable runs on to each of the others, and its **red** wire carries the signal. The circuit must not be controlled by a wall switch.

## Carbon monoxide alarms
Most CO alarms use an **electrochemical cell**: carbon monoxide that seeps in through a gas-permeable film reacts at an electrode and makes a small current, and the alarm sounds when the amount and the time add up to a danger. The cell wears out, so a CO alarm has an end-of-life date (usually 7 to 10 years) and chirps when it's reached.

## The smart home
Smart devices (locks, thermostats, lights, plugs, cameras, leak sensors) talk to a **hub** or to the home's Wi-Fi router, which reaches the internet through the **modem**, so a phone app can watch and control them from anywhere. Devices use different wireless languages, Wi-Fi, **Zigbee**, **Z-Wave** and Bluetooth among them; the **Matter** standard, launched in 2022, lets devices from different makers work together. Before buying, check that each device works with the hub or app you already use.

## Before calling an electrician
- **A light won't come on**: try a new bulb; then check its breaker. With the power off, gently pry the brass tab at the bottom of the socket up a little so it touches the bulb. Then suspect the switch.
- **A bulb turns with its socket**: turn off the breaker and take the fixture apart to separate them.
- **A bulb broke in the socket**: turn the power off, then press a raw potato or a rubber jar opener into the jagged base and twist it out.
- **A fluorescent tube flickers or won't start**: reseat the tube; replace the starter (if it has one), then the tube. Blackened ends mean the tube is near its end.
- **A smoke alarm chirps**: replace its battery; if it still won't test, or it's ten years old, replace the alarm with the same type.

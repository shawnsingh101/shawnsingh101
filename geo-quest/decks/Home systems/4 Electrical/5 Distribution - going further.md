The Electrical deep dive, part 2: inside the panel. How a breaker knows when to trip, fuses old and new, how the main panel and a subpanel are laid out, and what amps a circuit can safely carry.

## How a breaker trips
A standard breaker has two ways to trip, for two kinds of trouble:
- **Thermal**: a bimetal strip, two metals bonded together, bends as it heats. A moderate overload heats it slowly and it trips after seconds or minutes, which lets a motor's brief starting surge through.
- **Magnetic**: a coil around the current's path. A short circuit's huge current makes it pull a latch instantly.

## The main panel
The two hot legs of the service feed two **bus bars** down the middle of the panel; breakers snap onto them, alternating legs, so a double-pole breaker (two spaces) touches both and gets 240 volts. The **neutral bar** holds the white wires and the **ground bar** the bare ones; in the main panel they are bonded together and to the grounding electrode. A **subpanel** gets four wires (two hots, a neutral and a ground) and keeps its neutral bar isolated from its box.

## Fuses
- An old screw-in fuse is a {{plug fuse|which kind?}}; a window on top shows a melted strip or blackened glass when it has blown.
- A plug fuse with a narrow base that fits only one rating, so a larger fuse can't be swapped in, is a {{Type S|which kind?}} fuse.
- A long tube-shaped fuse, used for a main disconnect or a 240-volt circuit, is a {{cartridge fuse|which kind?}}.
- A slow-blow or {{time-delay|what kind?}} fuse rides out a motor's starting surge, like a breaker's thermal trip.

## Inside the panel
- The two metal strips down the middle of a panel, one for each hot leg, that the breakers snap onto are the {{bus bars|what?}}.
- A double-pole breaker takes two spaces so it touches {{both|one or both?}} hot bus bars, which gives 240 volts.
- The white wires end on the {{neutral bar|which bar?}}; the bare and green wires on the ground bar.
- In the main panel, the screw or strap that joins the neutral bar to the panel box is the {{main bonding jumper|what?}}.
- A subpanel is fed by four wires: two hots, a neutral and a {{ground|which wire?}}.
- A panel's label on the inside of its door, saying what each breaker feeds, is its {{circuit directory|what?}}.
- A panel must have {{36 inches|how much?}} of clear space in front of it for working.

## Wire, breaker and load
- A circuit's continuous load, like a heater running for hours, should stay under {{80 percent|how much?}} of its breaker's rating.
- On a 15-amp, 120-volt circuit, 80 percent is about {{1,440 watts|how much?}} of continuous load.
> $0.8 \times 15 \times 120 = 1440$ W: about one space heater.
- The two numbers on cable like "12/2 with ground" are the wire gauge and the number of {{insulated conductors|what?}}, not counting the bare ground.
- Cable labeled 14/3 with ground has a black, a white and a {{red|which color?}} wire, plus the bare ground.

## Why a dry house still gets wet
Cold winter air holds very little water, so when it leaks in and is warmed, its relative humidity drops and the house feels dry. But a household adds water all day: breathing and sweating, cooking, showers, laundry, plants, and above all a damp basement or crawl space, or a dryer vented indoors, each of which can add more than everything else together. When that air touches a surface colder than its **dew point**, such as a window, an outside corner or the underside of a roof, water condenses there. Mold needs only a damp surface and mild warmth, so it grows on those cold surfaces first. Insulating them, moving air past them and cutting the sources of moisture all help.

Humidity matters for health in both directions: very dry air dries out noses and throats and lets some viruses survive longer; damp air feeds **mold** and **dust mites**. The EPA's advice is to keep indoor humidity between about 30 and 50%, and below 60%.

## Humidifiers
- **Evaporative**: a fan blows air through a wet wick, pad or rotating belt. The water evaporates and the minerals stay behind on the pad.
- **Furnace (bypass)**: water trickles over a pad in a box on the furnace's ductwork, and warm air from the furnace passes through it.
- **Ultrasonic**: a vibrating plate throws a fine cool mist. With hard water it leaves a fine white dust of minerals on the furniture.
- **Steam**: boils the water, so it's the most sanitary but uses the most power.
Standing water grows bacteria and mold, so empty, scrub and dry the tank often.

## Dehumidifiers
![How a dehumidifier dries the air (drawn for this app)](figures/home/dehumidifier.svg)
A dehumidifier is an air conditioner that stays indoors. A fan pulls damp air across a cold **evaporator coil**; the air cools below its dew point and water condenses on the coil and drips into a **bucket** or out a **drain hose**. The same air then passes over the warm **condenser coil**, which heats it back up, so it leaves drier and a little warmer. Set it to about 50%; in a cold basement, frost on the coil means it's too cold for the machine.

## Filters and air cleaners
A cheap flat fiberglass filter, about an inch thick, protects the furnace's blower but catches only a small share of the fine particles that bother lungs. **Pleated** filters have much more surface and catch far more. Filters are rated by **MERV** from 1 to 16: MERV 8 to 13 suits most houses, but a very dense filter can choke the airflow of a furnace not built for it.

An **electronic air cleaner** catches particles with electricity: high-voltage wires give passing particles an electric charge, and a stack of oppositely charged **collector plates** pulls them out of the air, like a magnet. This is an **electrostatic precipitator**. The plates are washed when they look dusty: turn off the power and wait a minute (it holds a high voltage), soak the cell in warm water and detergent (or spray it), rinse gently without bending the thin plates or breaking the wires, and let it dry completely before putting it back.

## Fresh air without losing the heat
A well-sealed house needs fresh air brought in on purpose. The US ventilation standard (ASHRAE 62.2) asks for about 7.5 cubic feet a minute for each person plus 3 for each 100 square feet of floor. An **air-to-air heat exchanger**, or **heat recovery ventilator** (HRV), passes the outgoing stale air and incoming fresh air through a **core** of thin, alternating channels, so heat crosses from one stream to the other without the air mixing; it recovers most of the heat. An **energy recovery ventilator** (ERV) passes some moisture across too.

## Before calling for help
- **Weak airflow from the registers**: check the furnace filter. With the power off, open the access panel and look: a filter coated with dust is choking the air. Replace a cardboard-framed filter with the same size (buy several); a washable one with a plastic or metal frame can be hosed off and dried.
- **While the panel is open**: look at the blower belt, if there is one, and replace it if it's frayed or cracked.
- **A humidifier smells**: scrub and dry its tank. **Its airflow drops**: minerals have built up on the pad or belt: soak it in vinegar or replace it.

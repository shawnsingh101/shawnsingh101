## Why we feel cool
The body makes heat all the time and must lose it as fast as it makes it. It does so in four ways: **conduction** to what we touch, **convection** to the air moving past, **radiation** to cooler surfaces around us, and **evaporation** of sweat. So comfort depends on more than the air's temperature: humidity slows evaporation, sun and warm walls add radiation, and moving air speeds both convection and evaporation. That's why a fan cools *people* without cooling the room.

## Cooling without air conditioning
- **Cross-ventilation**: open windows on the side facing the breeze and on the opposite side. Casement windows and shrubs can catch and steer a breeze.
- **The stack effect**: warm air is lighter and rises, so air let in low and out high keeps flowing by itself, best on a still evening when the house is warmer than outdoors. For the most air through a house, make the inlets and outlets about the same size; for the fastest breeze through one window, make the outlets larger than the inlet.
- **A ceiling fan**: a breeze across the skin lets most people feel as comfortable with the thermostat about 4°F higher. It cools only the people under it, so turn it off when leaving the room. In summer, the blades should push air down (counterclockwise, seen from below).
- **A whole-house fan**: a big fan in the top-floor ceiling that pulls cool evening air in through open windows and pushes the hot air out through the attic. Close the house up in the morning, before it warms; open windows and run the fan once the outdoors cools in the evening. The attic needs plenty of vent area for the air to get out, and windows must be open before it runs, or it can pull exhaust back down a chimney or flue.

## Air conditioners
A window unit and a central system work alike: a cold **evaporator coil** indoors takes heat (and moisture) from the room, and a hot **condenser coil** outdoors gives it off (see the refrigerant loop in Cooling). In a window unit both sit in one box; the condensed water runs to the back and out, so the unit should tilt slightly **outward**. With central air, the evaporator sits on the furnace and shares its blower, ducts and filter; the condenser sits outside. Ducts in an attic or crawl space can lose a quarter or more of the cooling, which is one reason **ductless mini-splits** do well in additions, attics and converted garages.

A quick health check: the air coming out of a supply register should be about **15 to 20°F** cooler than the air going into the return. Less than that, and the filter or coils probably need cleaning, or the system needs service.

## Evaporative coolers
An evaporative ("swamp") cooler draws hot, dry outdoor air through pads kept wet by a small pump. Water evaporating from the pads takes heat from the air, which can come out 20°F or more cooler, but much more humid. It uses a fraction of an air conditioner's power, but it works only where the air is dry, as in the desert Southwest, and it needs a window or vent open for the air it brings in to leave by.

## Before calling for help
- **Doesn't start at all**: check the breakers (for central air, both the condenser's and the furnace's). If the furnace won't run either when you turn the heat up, suspect the thermostat or its wiring.
- **Runs, but the air isn't cool enough** (less than a 15°F drop): change the furnace filter; clean the indoor and outdoor coils. A window unit whose coil has iced over can be switched off for an hour to thaw.
- **A window unit drips into the room**: tilt it a little more to the outside.
- **A swamp cooler stops moving air**: check the breaker, then the blower's belt. **Moves air but cools poorly**: the pads are clogged with minerals from hard water; replace them.

The Heating deep dive, part 2: heaters that warm one room or one part of the house: vent-free and direct-vent gas heaters, gas fireplaces, pellet and wood stoves, the masonry fireplace and the stove that makes it efficient, and electric baseboard. ## Gas room heaters
A **vent-free** (ventless) gas heater burns its gas in the room and sends everything it makes into the room air: heat, and also water vapor and some carbon dioxide. That makes it nearly 100% efficient, but it adds a good deal of moisture, which a tight house can't get rid of, and its safety rests on burning cleanly. Every one has an **oxygen depletion sensor** (ODS): the pilot flame is tuned so that, if the oxygen in the room falls even a little, the flame lifts off the **thermocouple** in it, the thermocouple cools, and the gas valve closes. Some states and cities, California among them, don't allow vent-free heaters in homes.

A **direct-vent** heater is sealed from the room. A pipe-within-a-pipe through the wall brings outdoor air in through the outer pipe and sends the exhaust out through the inner one, so the incoming air is warmed by the outgoing exhaust and the room air never touches the flame. A **direct-vent gas fireplace** works the same way behind a sealed glass front, with ceramic logs over the burner.

## Pellet stoves
A pellet stove burns small pellets of compressed sawdust. An **auger** feeds them from a **hopper** into a small burn pot at a steady rate, a **combustion blower** pushes the air in and the exhaust out, and a second fan blows heat into the room. Because the exhaust is pushed by a fan rather than rising on its own, it can leave through a small (3- or 4-inch) **pellet vent** pipe, even straight out a wall. But the stove needs **electricity** to run: in a power outage it goes out. Pellets are dry and burn under control, so a pellet stove gives off far less smoke than a wood stove.

## Wood stoves and fireplaces
An **airtight** wood stove controls the air it lets in, so it burns slowly and steadily for hours. Modern stoves certified by the EPA burn the smoke itself, either by feeding hot air above the fire (**secondary combustion**) or passing the smoke through a **catalytic combustor**, so they make much less smoke and **creosote**. Creosote is the tar that condenses from cool smoke on the inside of a chimney; it's flammable, and a thick coat of it is how **chimney fires** start. Have a chimney inspected every year and swept as needed.

![A masonry fireplace, cut through (drawn for this app)](figures/home/fireplace.svg)
A traditional **masonry fireplace**, from the bottom: a **hearth** of fireproof masonry out into the room, a **firebox** lined with firebrick, a **damper** to close the throat when there's no fire, a **smoke shelf** behind it that turns back downdrafts and rain, the **smoke chamber**, the **flue** (a clay or stainless steel liner sized to the firebox), and a **cap** on top against rain, animals and sparks. It's a poor heater: it pulls warm house air up the chimney, and with the damper left open overnight for the embers it can lose more heat than it gave. An airtight stove or **fireplace insert** set into the fireplace and vented through a stainless steel **liner** run down the old flue can raise the efficiency to 60% or more.

## Electric baseboard
An electric baseboard is a heating element inside a row of metal fins. It turns electricity into heat at 100% efficiency, but electricity costs much more per unit of heat than gas or oil, and a heat pump gets two to four times as much heat from it. Most run on **240 volts**, controlled by a thermostat on the wall or on the unit itself. Keep furniture and drapes clear of it, and vacuum the fins each year.

## Before calling for help
- **A gas heater's pilot went out**: make sure the gas is on and, for propane, that the tank isn't empty; relight by the instructions on the heater. If it won't stay lit, call the dealer or gas supplier. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company from outside; don't light anything.
- **A baseboard won't heat**: turn the thermostat all the way up, then check its double breaker: switch it fully off and on again. Baseboards are cheap; a dead one can be replaced (with the breaker off) like for like.

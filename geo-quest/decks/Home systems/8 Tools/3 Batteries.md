The batteries around the house: the lead-acid car battery, alkaline cells, lithium-ion packs for phones and tools, and button cells, how each makes its voltage, how to read their ratings, and how to store and charge them so they last. The topics follow appendix A of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); facts are checked against Wikipedia and battery makers' data, and the cards are our own.

## Cells and batteries
A **cell** makes electricity from a chemical reaction between two electrodes in an **electrolyte**; a **battery** is strictly several cells connected together. In a **rechargeable** cell the reaction runs backward when a charger pushes current in. Each chemistry has its own voltage per cell, and a battery's **capacity** is given in **amp-hours** (Ah) or milliamp-hours: how long it can supply a current. Capacity always shrinks at high currents, so a battery drained fast delivers less than its rating.

## Lead-acid
A car battery has **six cells** in series, each about 2.1 volts when fully charged, so a healthy battery at rest reads about **12.6 volts**; about 12.0 volts means it is only half charged. The plates are lead and lead dioxide in sulfuric acid; older batteries needed water added, but today's are sealed, with the acid held in glass mat (**AGM**) or a gel. Ratings: **amp-hours**, usually over 20 hours, and for a car the **cold cranking amps** (CCA) it can deliver at 0°F to start the engine.

Lead-acid batteries discharge themselves faster the warmer they are, and every deep drain wears them, so a car stored for months should be kept on a **battery maintainer** (a smart float charger). A **starting** battery is built for short bursts; a **deep-cycle** battery (for a boat, RV or solar bank) for long, deep drains.

## Alkaline
The ordinary AA, AAA, C, D and 9-volt are **alkaline** cells: zinc and manganese dioxide, **1.5 volts** per cell (a 9-volt has six inside). They replaced the old carbon-zinc cell around 1960 with more energy, a longer **shelf life** (5 to 10 years) and less tendency to sag. They do poorly in high-drain gadgets like cameras, where lithium AAs or rechargeable nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) cells last longer. Take them out of anything stored for a long time: a spent alkaline can leak caustic potassium hydroxide.

## Lithium-ion
**Lithium-ion** cells power phones, laptops, cordless tools and electric cars. Charging moves lithium ions from the cathode into the graphite anode; discharging moves them back. Each cell is about **3.6 to 3.7 volts** (4.2 full), and lithium is so light that they pack several times the energy of lead-acid for their weight. Their voltage stays nearly flat until close to empty, so a tool keeps its power and then quits. Chargers use **constant current** and then **constant voltage** (CC-CV), and always the charger made for the battery. Heat ages them, and so does sitting full; for storage, about half charged in a cool place is best. A **swollen**, hot or damaged pack can catch fire: stop using it, and recycle lithium batteries rather than throwing them in the trash.

## Button cells
Watch and key-fob batteries are named by an international code: a letter for the chemistry (**C** lithium, 3 V; **L** alkaline, 1.5 V; **S** silver oxide, 1.55 V), **R** for round, then the **diameter in millimeters** and the **height in tenths of a millimeter**. A **CR2032** is a 3-volt lithium coin cell 20 mm across and 3.2 mm thick. (These lithium coin cells are not rechargeable lithium-ion.) An LR44 and an SR44 are the same size; silver oxide holds its voltage steadier, which watches like. A swallowed button battery can burn through a child's esophagus in about **two hours**: it's an emergency.

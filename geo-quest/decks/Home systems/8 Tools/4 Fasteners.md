The screws, bolts, nuts and washers that hold a house together, and how to ask for the right one: what the numbers mean, the common drives, and the anchors for drywall and concrete. The topics follow appendix B of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); sizes are checked against the Unified Thread Standard, and the cards are our own.

:::lesson
## Screws
- A **wood screw** (and its modern cousins, deck and drywall screws) cuts its own thread in a soft material. It is named by **head**, **gauge** and **length**: a "#8 × 1½ flat head" is gauge 8 (about 0.164 inch across), an inch and a half long. Gauges run from #2 to #14; #8 and #10 are the everyday sizes. A flat (countersunk) head is measured overall; a round or pan head from under the head.
- A **machine screw** goes into a threaded hole or a nut. Its name adds the **threads per inch**: a "#8-32 × 2" is gauge 8, 32 threads per inch, 2 inches long. The screws that hold outlets and switches to their boxes are #6-32.

## Bolts and nuts
A **bolt** passes through drilled parts and is tightened with a **nut**. Above #12, sizes are given in fractions of an inch: a "¼-20 × 3 hex bolt" is ¼ inch across, 20 threads per inch, 3 inches long. Most sizes come in two threads under the **Unified Thread Standard**: **coarse** (UNC), the common one, and **fine** (UNF): a ⅜-inch bolt is 16 threads per inch coarse or 24 fine. A nut must match both the diameter and the thread. Metric bolts give the diameter and the distance between threads in millimeters: M8 × 1.25. A **thread gauge** at the hardware store settles any doubt.

## Drives and washers
The oldest drive is the **slotted** screw, easy to make but hard to keep a screwdriver in. P. L. Robertson's **square** drive (1908, still standard in Canada) holds the bit firmly; the **hex socket** (Allen) followed; and Henry Phillips's **cross** drive, patented in the 1930s, conquered the car industry because a power driver can't over-tighten it: the bit **cams out** instead. The six-lobed **Torx** drive (1967) transmits the most turning force without slipping. A set of slotted and Phillips bits plus hex keys covers most of a house.

**Washers** spread a nut's or screw head's load over a soft surface (a wide **fender washer** spreads it further), protect the surface from a turning nut, and, as split or toothed **lock washers**, keep it from vibrating loose. A **nylon-insert lock nut** does that job by itself.

## Anchors
The best way to hang something heavy is a screw into a **stud**. Where there's none:
- **Plastic expansion anchors**: tapped into a drilled hole, they spread as the screw goes in; for light loads.
- **Self-drilling drywall anchors**: big coarse threads screw straight into drywall; light to medium loads.
- **Molly bolts**: a metal sleeve that collapses behind the drywall as the screw is tightened.
- **Toggle bolts**: spring-loaded wings that open behind the wall, for heavy loads in hollow walls.
- **Concrete screws** (like Tapcon) in a hole drilled with a masonry bit, or **sleeve** and **wedge anchors** for heavy loads in concrete and brick.
Tell the hardware store what you're hanging and on what: there's a fastener for nearly every job.

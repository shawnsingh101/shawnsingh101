The Framing deep dive: the loads a frame must carry, how a beam bends and how it fails, the engineered lumber that does more with less wood, how joists, beams, rafters and trusses are sized, and five ways of framing a house, from the timber frame to advanced framing. Order follows chapter 8 of *How Your House Works* (Charlie Wing); numbers are checked against the International Residential Code (IRC), and the cards and drawing are our own.

:::lesson
## The forces on a frame
- **Dead load**: the weight of the building itself, its framing, sheathing, drywall and roofing.
- **Live load**: what occupancy adds. The IRC designs living areas for **40 pounds per square foot**, sleeping rooms for 30, and an attic with limited storage for 20.
- **Snow load**: the weight of the deepest snow expected, about once in 50 years, on the roof; it ranges from nothing in the South to well over 100 psf in the mountains, so the local building department sets it.
- **Wind**: pressure on the upwind walls and **uplift** on the roof, set by the local design wind speed and the **exposure** (open country and shorelines get more than a sheltered suburb). Hurricane ties and hold-downs exist to keep the roof and walls from lifting off.

## How a beam bends
![How a beam carries a load (drawn for this app)](figures/home/beam-bending.svg)
A loaded beam sags. Its top fibers are squeezed (**compression**) and its bottom fibers stretched (**tension**); the middle, the **neutral axis**, does little. A long beam usually fails when its **bottom fibers pull apart**, so a knot, notch or hole near the bottom edge in the middle of the span weakens it most. A short, heavily loaded beam can instead fail in **shear**: the layers slide past each other along the grain, splitting it lengthwise, worst near the supports.

Strength is not the only test. A floor can be strong enough and still bounce, so the code also limits **deflection**, the sag under the live load, as a fraction of the span: **L/360** for floors, L/240 for ceilings under drywall, L/180 for rafters with no ceiling. A beam's stiffness rises with the **cube** of its depth: a joist twice as deep is eight times stiffer, which is why joists stand on edge.

## Engineered wood
- **Plywood**: thin veneers glued with the grain of each layer crossing the one before, so it is strong in both directions and doesn't split.
- **Oriented strand board (OSB)**: flakes of wood in crossed layers, pressed with resin; it does plywood's job for less.
Both are **structural panels**: strong in shear, they sheathe floors, walls and roofs and **brace** the walls against racking.
- **I-joists**: two flanges of solid lumber or LVL joined by an OSB web, an I-beam in wood. They are light, long, straight and don't shrink, and holes may be cut in the web only where the maker's chart allows.
- **Glulam**: many layers of lumber glued into a big beam, the best layers placed at the top and bottom where the stress is greatest. **LVL** (laminated veneer lumber) does the same with veneers.

## Joists, beams, rafters and trusses
Joists and rafters are sized from the code's **span tables**: the allowed span depends on the load, the species and grade of the lumber, and the spacing (12, 16 or 24 inches **on center**). Beams carry other members: the **girder** down the middle of a basement, the **header** over a window or door. A **truss** is a frame of triangles; the triangle is the only shape that can't change without a member changing length, so the top chords work in compression, the bottom chord in tension, and light 2×4s can span a whole house. That is also why **no truss member may be cut**.

Notches and holes in sawn joists follow code rules: a notch no deeper than a sixth of the joist, and none in the middle third of the span; a hole no bigger than a third of the depth and at least 2 inches from the top and bottom edges.

## Five ways to frame a house
- **Timber frame** (post and beam): big hewn timbers joined with **mortise-and-tenon** joints locked by wooden pegs, the way houses and barns were built before cheap sawn lumber and nails.
- **Plank and beam**: exposed beams a few feet apart covered by 2-inch-thick tongue-and-groove planks that are both floor and ceiling.
- **Balloon frame**: long studs running unbroken from the sill to the roof, made possible by sawn lumber and machine-made nails in the 1830s. The tall open stud bays let fire race between floors; it faded out by the 1940s, and the code now requires **fireblocking** in such cavities.
- **Platform frame**: each floor is built as a deck, and the next story's walls stand on it. Shorter studs, a ready work surface and built-in fire stops made it the standard in the 1940s, with 4×8 sheathing and 16- or 24-inch spacing.
- **Advanced framing** (optimum value engineering, OVE): research for HUD in the 1970s asked "why two sticks when one will do?" Studs, joists and rafters line up at 24 inches on center, walls get single top plates, two-stud corners and headers only where loads need them. It saves lumber and leaves more room for insulation.

## Before cutting into the frame
- Before removing or opening a wall, find out what it carries: a wall under the middle of the joists, or with a beam or post above or below it, is probably **load-bearing**. Ask an engineer or builder before cutting one.
- Drill holes near the middle of a joist's depth, never notch the bottom edge in mid-span, and never cut a truss.
- A bouncy floor can often be stiffened with **blocking** between joists or a new mid-span beam.

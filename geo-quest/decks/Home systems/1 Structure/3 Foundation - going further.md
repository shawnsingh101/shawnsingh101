## Footings and soils
A footing's only job is to spread the house's weight over enough soil that the house never moves. Three rules make that happen: it rests on **undisturbed** (or properly compacted) soil, it is **wide enough** that the pressure stays under what the soil can carry, and it sits **below the frost line**.

The IRC gives each soil a presumed **load-bearing value**, in pounds per square foot:

| Soil | Bearing, psf |
| --- | --- |
| Crystalline bedrock | 12,000 |
| Sedimentary rock | 4,000 |
| Sandy gravel and gravel | 3,000 |
| Sand, silty and clayey sand and gravel | 2,000 |
| Clay, silt and their mixes | 1,500 |

The weaker the soil and the heavier the house (more stories, brick veneer), the wider the footing; the code's minimum for any footing is **12 inches** wide and 6 inches thick. Clay is the troublemaker: it carries the least, and some clays swell when wet and shrink when dry.

## Keeping a basement dry
![Seven things that keep a basement dry (drawn for this app)](figures/home/foundation-drainage.svg)
Unless it's a walkout or sits in deep gravel, a basement is a hole in the ground that wants to fill with water. Seven things keep it dry, from the roof down:
1. **Gutters** catch the roof's water.
2. **Downspouts and extensions** let it out 4 to 6 feet from the wall.
3. A **cap of clay soil** at the surface, sloped away (6 inches of fall in the first 10 feet), sheds rain.
4. **Free-draining backfill** (gravel or sand) against the wall lets any water that gets in drop straight down instead of pressing on the wall.
5. **Damp-proofing** (a coating) or, where groundwater is high, a **waterproofing** membrane on the outside of the wall.
6. **Crushed stone** around the drain, wrapped in **filter fabric** so silt can't clog it.
7. A **perforated drain pipe** beside the footing, sloped to daylight or to a sump pit.

## Radon
![Venting radon from under the slab (drawn for this app)](figures/home/radon-mitigation.svg)
**Radon** is a radioactive gas from the natural decay of uranium in soil and rock. It has no color or smell, seeps into houses through floors and foundations, and is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. The EPA says to **test** every home (cheap kits, or a digital monitor) and to fix it at **4 picocuries per liter** (pCi/L) or more, and to consider fixing it between 2 and 4.

The usual fix is **sub-slab depressurization**: a pipe through the slab into the gravel below, a **fan** that keeps the space under the slab at slightly lower pressure than the house, and an exhaust **above the roof**. The fan goes in the attic, garage or outside, never in the living space, because any leak on its pressure side would blow radon indoors. Sealing cracks and floor joints makes it work better, and a U-tube **manometer** on the pipe shows it's running. In a crawlspace, a heavy plastic sheet sealed over the dirt plays the part of the slab.

## Five ways to found a house
- **Slab-on-grade**: a concrete slab, at least 3½ inches thick, on 4 inches of gravel and a **polyethylene vapor retarder**, reinforced with wire mesh or bars and thickened at the edges. Rigid foam at the edge keeps a heated slab warm. A **frost-protected shallow foundation** adds a skirt of foam insulation sloping out from the edge, so the ground under the footing never freezes and the footing can be shallow even in cold climates.
- **Crawlspace**: short walls on footings below the frost line. The dirt floor gets a sealed **vapor barrier**; the crawlspace is either vented to the outside or, better in most climates, sealed and conditioned like a short basement.
- **Full basement**: the walls hold up the house and hold back the soil, so they get reinforcing, damp-proofing, drainage and often insulation.
- **Grade beam**: a reinforced concrete beam at ground level resting on deep **piers** or piles instead of a continuous footing; it suits steep slopes and unstable or swelling soils.
- **Pier**: separate posts or piers, each on its own footing below the frost line; the cheapest way to lift a cabin, porch or deck off the ground. The floor above needs its own insulation.

Whatever the type, **anchor bolts** (½ inch, set at least 7 inches into the concrete, no more than 6 feet apart and within a foot of each end of a sill plate) tie the house to it.

## Before calling for help
- **A wet basement**: start at the top of the list: clean the gutters, extend the downspouts, and rebuild the slope of the soil before paying for drains or pumps.
- **Test for radon** with a short-term kit; if it reads 4 pCi/L or more, test again, then call a certified radon mitigator.
- **Check a radon system** now and then: the liquid in the manometer should stay uneven, and the fan should hum.
- **Cracks**: thin vertical or diagonal shrinkage cracks in a basement wall are common; cracks that are wider at one end, horizontal, or growing call for a structural engineer.

## The double-hung window
A double-hung window has two **sashes** that slide past each other in the frame. In an old wood window each sash hangs from a **sash cord** that runs over a **pulley** at the top of the frame to a cast-iron **weight** hidden in a pocket in the wall, so the sash stays wherever it is put. Where the two sashes meet, their **meeting rails** are beveled to close tightly, and a **sash lock** pulls them together. The glass is held in with small metal **glazing points** and sealed with **glazing putty**. Modern windows replace the cords and weights with spring **balances** in the side jambs, and most are vinyl, aluminum or fiberglass with sealed double glazing.

Old wood windows can be kept going for decades: strip, paint and reputty them, add weatherstripping, and they last far longer than a vinyl window. They are worth keeping in an old house; the empty weight pockets, though, are a path for air and heat.

## What low-E glass does
Sunlight is a spread of wavelengths: about half its energy is **visible light**, a little is **ultraviolet** (which fades fabrics), and the rest is short-wave **infrared**, felt as heat. Everything in a warm room also glows, at much longer infrared wavelengths we can't see. Clear glass lets most sunlight through but blocks that long-wave glow poorly, so heat leaks out at night.

A **low-emissivity (low-E)** coating is a microscopically thin metal or metal-oxide layer that reflects long-wave infrared, keeping room heat in. Coatings come in two flavors:
- **Hard-coat** (pyrolytic) low-E, baked on at the glass factory, still lets most of the sun's heat in: good where heating bills dominate.
- **Spectrally selective** (soft-coat, sputtered silver) low-E passes visible light but turns back most of the sun's infrared too: good where cooling bills dominate.

The window's **NFRC label** gives the numbers: the **U-factor** (how fast heat leaks through; lower is better), the **solar heat gain coefficient** (SHGC, the share of the sun's heat let in, from 0 to 1) and the **visible transmittance** (how much light). Pick a low U-factor everywhere, a higher SHGC in cold climates and a low SHGC in hot ones.

## An add-on insulating panel
Heat flow through a window is inversely proportional to its R-value. Single glass is about **R-1** and double glazing about **R-2**; a tight-fitting interior panel (clear plastic film or acrylic on a light frame, sealed with foam weatherstrip) traps another layer of still air and roughly doubles or triples the R-value, cutting the window's heat loss by half to two-thirds. Shrink-film kits do the same for a winter.

## How a key turns a lock
Most door locks are **pin tumbler** locks, the design Linus Yale Jr. perfected in the 1860s. A cylinder (the **plug**) turns inside a fixed housing. Five or six holes run through both; in each, a **key pin** and a **driver pin** are pushed down by a small **spring**, and the drivers straddle the gap between plug and housing, so the plug can't turn. The right key lifts every pin pair so its split lines up exactly with that gap, the **shear line**, and the plug turns. A **tailpiece** (cam) on the back of the plug works the latch or bolt.

A **keyed knob** has a spring **latch** with a beveled end, so the door latches by itself when it's closed; but the bevel can be pushed back with a thin blade. A **deadbolt** has a square-ended bolt, thrown by the key or the inside **thumb turn**, that can't be pushed back. For the bolt to hold, the **strike plate** needs long screws (about 3 inches) that reach the wall framing, not just the door jamb.

## The garage door opener
![A garage door opener, from the side (drawn for this app)](figures/home/garage-door-opener.svg)
The door is a set of hinged **sections** with rollers running in **tracks** at each side. Big **springs** do the lifting: either a **torsion spring** on a shaft above the door or **extension springs** along the tracks, set to balance nearly all of the door's weight. So the opener only steers it: a **motor** drives a chain, belt or screw in an overhead **rail**, which pulls a **trolley**, and an **arm** links the trolley to the top of the door.

Two safety systems have been required on every opener sold in the US since 1993 (the UL 325 standard): **photo eyes** on each side of the opening, no more than 6 inches above the floor, which reverse a closing door if the beam is broken, and a **force sensor** that reverses the door if it meets more resistance than it should. A red **emergency release** cord frees the door from the trolley so it can be worked by hand in a power outage.

## Before calling for help
- **A sash won't stay up** (broken cord): prop it, or pin it open with a nail through a hole drilled through the sash side into the frame; the same trick in the closed position makes a lock.
- **Broken glass in a wood sash**: soften the old putty with a heat gun, scrape it out, pull the glazing points, set in new glass cut to size, new points, new putty; paint the putty after it skins over.
- **Fogged double glazing in a vinyl window**: pry out the snap-in **glazing beads** and take the sealed unit to a glass shop for an exact copy.
- **A torn screen**: pull out the rubber **spline**, lay new screening, and roll in new spline of the same diameter with a spline roller.
- **Vinyl color**: vinyl can be painted with an exterior acrylic latex after a good cleaning; check the window maker's warranty first.
- **Garage door won't close**: look for something blocking the photo eyes, check their lights, and wipe the lenses; realign them if one light blinks.
- **Test the door monthly**: lay a 2×4 flat on the floor under the door; a closing door must reverse when it touches it.
- **Never adjust or replace a torsion spring yourself**: it stores enough energy to maim; that's a job for a garage door technician.

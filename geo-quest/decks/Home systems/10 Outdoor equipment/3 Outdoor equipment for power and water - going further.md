## How much power do you need?
A whole-house standby generator, wired in and fueled by natural gas or propane, can run everything but costs many thousands of dollars installed. Most people need much less to ride out an outage: some lights and phone charging; then the **refrigerator** and freezer; then a window air conditioner or the furnace blower; then a well pump. Add up the **running watts** of what you'll use at once, and remember that a motor (a refrigerator, a pump, an air conditioner) needs two to three times its running watts for a moment to **start**.

## Two portable choices
- An **inverter generator** (about 2,000 watts is common) runs on gasoline, throttles down at light loads to save fuel, is fairly quiet, and makes clean power safe for electronics. Its small tank lasts a few hours at a heavy load, so a long outage means keeping gallons of stabilized or ethanol-free gas on hand, and running it every few months.
- A **portable power station** is a lithium battery (often lithium iron phosphate) with a built-in inverter. It is silent, makes no fumes, and can sit indoors; its capacity is in **watt-hours**. Runtime is roughly the watt-hours divided by the watts drawn, less about 10 to 15% for the inverter: 1,000 Wh runs a 100-watt load for about 8 or 9 hours. It recharges from the wall, a car, solar panels or a generator.

## Connecting to the house safely
A generator's exhaust holds **carbon monoxide** that can kill in minutes. Run it **outside only**, at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away from windows, doors and vents, and keep a battery CO alarm working inside. Plug appliances into it with heavy outdoor **extension cords**, or have an electrician install a **transfer switch** or a panel **interlock** with a power **inlet** box outside. Either one makes it impossible for the generator and the utility to feed the house at the same time. Plugging a generator into a wall outlet with a double-ended cord **backfeeds** the utility lines, where the transformer steps it up to thousands of volts: it can kill a line worker, and it is illegal.

## The lawn sprinkler system
Water comes from the house supply through a shutoff and a **backflow preventer** (a pressure vacuum breaker or a double check valve), required so that yard water, fertilizer and all, can't be drawn back into the drinking water. Buried pipe runs to **zones**, each controlled by an electric **valve**. The **controller** (timer) sends 24 volts AC to one zone's valve at a time; its **solenoid** lifts a plunger, the pressure inside the valve lets its diaphragm open, and that zone's heads run. **Pop-up spray heads** rise on water pressure and drop flush with the lawn when it stops; **rotors** throw a stream back and forth over a wider circle. Flexible **swing joints** between the pipe and the heads keep a mower or a car from snapping the pipe. Water early in the morning, deeply and not too often. In cold climates, the system is **blown out** with compressed air every fall so water can't freeze in the pipes.

## The pool pump and filter
![A pool's water loop (drawn for this app)](figures/home/pool-pump.svg)
The pump pulls water from the **skimmer** at the water line (its basket catches leaves) and the **main drain** at the bottom, through a **strainer basket** on the front of the pump that catches hair and lint. It pushes the water through the **filter**: a tank of sand (cleaned by **backwashing**, running water backward through it), a pleated **cartridge** (hosed off), or **diatomaceous earth** (DE), the finest. The water goes back to the pool through the return **jets**. A **pressure gauge** on the filter tells when to clean it: when it reads about 8 to 10 psi above its clean reading. The pump should run long enough to filter the whole pool about once a day. Since 2021, federal efficiency rules have made most new full-size pool pumps **variable-speed**: running slower for longer uses far less electricity. The pump must be on a **GFCI**, as all pool equipment is.

## Before calling for help
- **Generator**: start it every month or two under a load, and keep its fuel stabilized.
- **No sprinklers run at all**: check that the water supply valve is open, the controller is plugged in and its breaker on, the **rain sensor** isn't holding it off, and the program and clock are right.
- **One zone won't run**: with a meter, check for 24 volts AC at the controller's terminal for that zone while it's on. No voltage: the controller. Voltage there: the wire, or the valve's **solenoid**, which unscrews and can be replaced.
- **A head sprays wildly or not at all**: clean its nozzle and screen, or replace it.
- **Pool pressure gauge high and flow weak**: clean or backwash the filter, and empty the skimmer and pump baskets.
- **Pump hums but won't start**: switch it off; a seized motor or a failed **capacitor** needs service.

How consumption and saving respond to current income, future income, permanent changes, wealth and the real interest rate, and how that adds up to a national saving curve. Lecture 7 of Macroeconomics for Public Policy.

:::lesson
## Beyond log utility
The log-utility solution showed that consumption and saving depend on current income, future income, wealth, the real interest rate and patience. To see which way each one pushes without assuming a particular utility function, assume only that consumption in both periods is a **normal good**: more resources mean more of both.

## A rise in current income
A higher $Y_1$ moves the endowment point right without changing the slope: the budget line shifts out in parallel. With both periods normal, the consumer spreads the gain: $C_1$ and $C_2$ both rise. Since $C_1$ rises by less than $Y_1$, saving rises too. This is **consumption smoothing**: a temporary windfall is partly saved for later.

The **marginal propensity to consume** is the extra consumption from one more unit of current income, and the **marginal propensity to save** is the rest:

$$MPC = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}, \qquad MPS = 1 - MPC, \qquad 0 < MPC < 1$$

Adding up across households, aggregate consumption and national saving both rise with current income.

A consumer facing a **binding borrowing constraint** is different: they'd like to borrow, so extra current income goes straight into consumption. For them the MPC can be 1. This is one reason tax rebates to poorer households move spending more.

## A rise in future income
A higher $Y_2$ moves the endowment point up: again a parallel shift out. Both $C_1$ and $C_2$ rise (people consume more today on the strength of a better future), but current income hasn't changed, so saving **falls**. Expecting a raise, you save less now.

## Permanent and temporary
Milton Friedman's **permanent income hypothesis**: a permanent rise in income (both $Y_1$ and $Y_2$) raises current consumption more than a temporary one, since there's nothing to smooth. The MPC out of a permanent change can be close to 1.

Franco Modigliani's **life-cycle model** extends this over a lifetime: income starts low, peaks in the decade before retirement, then falls. To smooth consumption people borrow when young, save in midlife and dissave in retirement. It predicts the old have a higher MPC than the young, and that an aging population saves less.

## A rise in wealth
More wealth shifts the budget line out like current income, but it isn't current income, so its effect on saving is like future income's: $C_1$ and $C_2$ rise, and saving falls. The effect is spread over many years, so a stock market or housing boom raises consumption, but far less than one-for-one.

## A rise in the real interest rate
Holding incomes fixed with no wealth, a higher $r$ leaves the endowment point in place but makes the budget line **steeper**: it rotates clockwise around the endowment point. Two effects follow:
- **Substitution effect**: consuming today now costs more future goods, so $C_1$ falls and $C_2$ rises, for everyone.
- **Income effect**: lenders earn more on their saving and are richer, so they consume more of both. Borrowers pay more and are poorer, so they consume less of both.

| | Substitution | Income | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lender: $C_1$ | down | up | ambiguous |
| Lender: $S_1$ | up | down | ambiguous |
| Borrower: $C_1$ | down | down | down |
| Borrower: $S_1$ | up | up | up |

A third channel is the **wealth effect**. Assets with a fixed future payout, like long-term bonds, lose present value when $r$ rises, which shrinks the budget and pushes consumption down and saving up. Assets whose payout adjusts, like bank deposits or floating-rate notes, keep their value and have no wealth effect.

## The national saving curve
Add it all up. Empirically, substitution and wealth effects usually outweigh the income effect, so **national saving rises with $r$**: an upward-sloping curve on a graph with $r$ up the side. The stronger those effects, the flatter it is.

A change in $r$ is a **movement along** the curve. Anything else that changes saving at every $r$ **shifts** it: a rise in $Y_1$ shifts it right; a rise in $Y_2$ or in wealth shifts it left; demographics, preferences and the distribution of income shift it too.

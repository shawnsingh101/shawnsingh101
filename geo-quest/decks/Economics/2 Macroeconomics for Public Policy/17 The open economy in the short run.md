## Net exports in the IS curve
In an open economy, desired spending includes net exports, $NX(Y, Y_{For}, e)$: they fall with home income (more imports), rise with foreign income, and fall with the real exchange rate. So the IS curve shifts **right** when foreign income rises, when the currency depreciates, or when tastes shift toward home goods.

## Flexible exchange rates
- **Monetary expansion**: LM shifts right, the domestic rate falls, capital flows out and the currency **depreciates**. Net exports rise, so IS shifts right too: monetary policy is **stronger** in an open economy with a floating currency.
- **Fiscal expansion**: IS shifts right and the rate rises, drawing capital in. The currency **appreciates** and net exports fall, offsetting part of the boost: crowding out through trade.

```graph
IS: down
LM: up
FE: vertical 0.5
shift LM: right
shift IS: right
equilibrium: IS, LM
```

## Fixed exchange rates
To hold a fixed rate with capital free to move, the central bank must keep the domestic interest rate in line with the anchor country's. It can't use monetary policy for its own goals: any attempt sends capital in or out and forces it to reverse course. **Fiscal policy**, though, is strong: it doesn't move the currency. A country can **devalue** to raise net exports, but expected devaluations bring **speculative attacks** that drain reserves (Britain in 1992, Thailand in 1997).

## The impossible trinity
A country can have only two of three: a **fixed exchange rate**, **free capital movement**, and an **independent monetary policy**. The euro area keeps fixed rates and free capital and gives up national monetary policy; the United States floats; China long kept capital controls.

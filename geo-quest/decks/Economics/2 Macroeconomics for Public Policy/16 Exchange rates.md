## Nominal and real
The **nominal exchange rate** $e_{nom}$ is the price of a currency: here, units of foreign currency per unit of home currency (for the US, yen per dollar). A rise is an **appreciation** of the home currency; a fall, a **depreciation**.

What matters for trade is the **real exchange rate**: how many foreign goods one home good buys.

$$e = \frac{e_{nom}\,P}{P_{For}}$$

A higher real exchange rate makes home goods dear abroad and foreign goods cheap at home, so **net exports fall**.

## Purchasing power parity
If the same goods sold everywhere at one price, $e$ would be 1: **purchasing power parity (PPP)**, so $e_{nom} = P_{For}/P$. Then currencies of high-inflation countries depreciate by about the inflation gap (**relative PPP**). It holds only roughly and over long periods: many goods and services aren't traded, and there are transport costs and tariffs. *The Economist*'s **Big Mac index** is a famous test.

## Interest parity
Investors compare returns across currencies. Holding the home currency earns $i$; holding the foreign one earns $i_{For}$ plus whatever the home currency is expected to lose:

$$i = i_{For} - \frac{\Delta e_{nom}^e}{e_{nom}}$$

So a currency expected to depreciate must pay a **higher interest rate**: **uncovered interest parity**.

## What moves currencies
In the long run: prices (PPP) and productivity. In the short run: anything that raises demand for home assets or goods. Higher domestic real interest rates draw in capital and **appreciate** the currency; so do higher foreign demand for home goods and a rise in home productivity. Fixed vs flexible: a **fixed** exchange rate needs the central bank to buy and sell its currency at the set price, using its **reserves**; a **devaluation** lowers a fixed rate, a **revaluation** raises it.

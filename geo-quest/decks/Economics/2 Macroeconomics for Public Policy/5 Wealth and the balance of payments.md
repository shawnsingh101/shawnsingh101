title: Wealth and the balance of payments

How people and nations build wealth: assets and liabilities, saving, national income, and the balance of payments that links a country's trade to what it owns abroad. Lecture 5 of Macroeconomics for Public Policy.

:::lesson
## Wealth is a stock
**Income** is a flow: earned over a period. **Wealth**, or net worth, is a stock, measured at a moment:

$$Wealth = Assets - Liabilities$$

An **asset** is something owned that will provide benefits in the future; a **liability** is something owed. **Real assets** are physical: homes, land, natural resources, the capital stock. Some are produced (machines), some aren't (land). **Financial assets** are claims on someone else's income, a promise written down: a bond, a share, a bank deposit. Every financial asset is someone else's liability.

## How wealth changes
**Saving** is income minus current spending, where current spending is everything except the purchase of assets. Whatever is saved must go into assets or into paying down debt. Someone who earns 100,000 dollars and spends 80,000 on current needs saves 20,000 and owns 20,000 more in assets (or owes 20,000 less).

Wealth also changes when the prices of what's already owned or owed change:

$$\Delta Wealth = Saving + \Delta\text{price of existing assets and liabilities}$$

If that same person's house loses 50,000 in value, their wealth falls by 30,000 despite saving 20,000.

## A nation's wealth
For a country, wealth is its capital stock plus its **net foreign assets**: what residents own abroad ($FA$, foreign bonds, deposits in foreign banks, foreign currency) minus what foreigners own here ($FL$, domestic bonds, shares and deposits held by foreigners):

$$Wealth = K + (FA - FL), \qquad NFA = FA - FL$$

## National income and national saving
A nation's income is not GDP but **gross national disposable income**:

$$GNDI = Y + NI + NUT$$

GDP plus **net primary income** from abroad ($NI$: wages, interest and profits in, minus out) plus **net unilateral transfers** ($NUT$, or secondary income: remittances and aid, in minus out). Spending on goods and services is **absorption**, $ABS = C + I + G$. Treating all of $G$ as current spending (not quite right, since it includes public investment), national saving is what's left:

```derive
# Where national saving goes
S = GNDI - (C + G) :: National saving is national income minus current spending.
S = Y + NI + NUT - (C + G) :: Write out national income.
S = (C + I + G + NX) + NI + NUT - (C + G) :: Replace GDP with the expenditure approach.
S = I + NX + NI + NUT :: C and G cancel.
```

In a closed economy $NX$, $NI$ and $NUT$ are zero, so $S = I$: the only way to save is to build capital.

## The balance of payments
The **balance of payments** records all of a country's transactions with the rest of the world over a period: in goods, services, income and assets.
- The **current account** ($CA$): net exports of goods and services, net primary income, and net secondary income: $CA = NX + NI + NUT$.
- The **capital account** ($KA$): non-produced, non-financial assets (land, mineral or fishing rights) and capital transfers like debt forgiveness. Usually small.
- The **financial account**: changes in foreign financial assets ($\Delta FA$) and liabilities ($\Delta FL$), by type: **foreign direct investment** (at least 10% of the votes: lasting control), **portfolio investment** (bonds and shares, easy to sell), financial derivatives, other investment (deposits, loans, trade credit) and the central bank's **reserves**.

Every transaction has two sides, so:

$$CA + KA = \Delta FA - \Delta FL$$

When China sells 10 of goods to the US and buys 1, its current account is $+9$, and it must end up holding 9 more in claims on the US, such as dollars or Treasury bonds. The US side mirrors it: $CA = -9$, $\Delta FL = +9$. In practice the two sides are measured separately, and the gap is filled by **errors and omissions**.

A warning on terms: the IMF's current manual (BPM6) writes it as above. The older one (BPM5) wrote $CA + KA - \Delta FA + \Delta FL = 0$ and called the last three terms together the "capital and financial account". Both say the same thing.

## Flows and stocks, together
With no capital account, no errors and no price changes:

$$\Delta NFA = CA = NX + NI + NUT = S - I = GNDI - ABS$$

A country adds to its net foreign assets when it runs a current account surplus, which is the same as saving more than it invests, and the same as earning more than it spends. A deficit means borrowing from abroad, or selling assets to foreigners.

The actual stock is reported in the **net international investment position**, which also moves with exchange rates and asset prices that the balance of payments doesn't record. So in full: $\Delta Wealth = \Delta K + \Delta NFA = S + \Delta\text{prices of existing } FA, FL$, minus depreciation of capital.

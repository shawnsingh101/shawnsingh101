## Deriving labor demand
- With $Y = K^{1/2}N^{1/2}$ and $K = 1$, $MPN = \tfrac{1}{2}N^{-1/2}$; setting it equal to $w$ gives $N^D = {{\dfrac{1}{4w^2}}}$.
eq: Labor demand | N^D = \left(\frac{(1-\alpha)A}{w}\right)^{1/\alpha} K
- The general Cobb-Douglas labor demand: $N^D = \left(\dfrac{(1-\alpha)A}{w}\right)^{ {{1/\alpha}} } K$
eq: Labor demand | N^D = \left(\frac{(1-\alpha)A}{w}\right)^{1/\alpha} K
- The wage elasticity of Cobb-Douglas labor demand is $-1/\alpha$; with $\alpha = 1/2$ it is {{-2|what number?}}.
- Starting from 31.7 million workers, a 1% rise in the real wage with an elasticity of $-2$ leaves firms wanting about {{31.1 million|how many?}} workers.

## Shocks on the graph
- EU workers leave the UK: UK employment falls by less than the number who left, because the higher real wage {{draws more of the remaining workers into jobs|why?}}.
- After that exodus, the income of capital owners {{falls|rises or falls?}}, since each unit of capital now works with less labor.
- After a productivity shock, a more elastic labor supply means the real wage rises {{less|more or less?}}.
- A productivity shock raises the return to capital for two reasons: higher TFP and {{higher employment|what else?}}.
- Firms move capital out of the country: labor demand shifts {{left|which way?}}, pushing the real wage down.

title: The IS-LM-FE model
---
Putting the goods, money and labor markets together: the IS curve, the LM curve, full-employment output, and what happens when prices are slow to adjust. Lecture 13 of Macroeconomics for Public Policy.

:::lesson
## Three markets, three curves
All three are drawn with output $Y$ across and the real interest rate $r$ up.

- **FE** (full employment): output when the labor market clears, $\bar{Y} = A F(K, \bar{N})$. It doesn't depend on $r$, so it's **vertical**. A productivity rise or more labor shifts it right.
- **IS** (investment = saving): the goods market. A higher $r$ means less investment and more saving, which is consistent only with a lower $Y$. So IS **slopes down**. Anything that raises desired spending at a given $r$ (more government purchases, confidence, expected future productivity) shifts it **right**.
- **LM** (liquidity = money): the money market, $\frac{M}{P} = L(Y, r + \pi^e)$. Higher income raises money demand; to keep it equal to a fixed supply, $r$ must rise. So LM **slopes up**. More real money ($M$ up or $P$ down) shifts it **right**.

```graph
# General equilibrium
axes: Y | r
IS: down
LM: up
FE: vertical 0.55
equilibrium: IS, LM
```

## Getting back to full employment
When IS and LM cross off the FE line, the **price level** does the work. If they cross to the right of FE, the economy is overheating: prices rise, $M/P$ falls, LM shifts left until all three meet. To the left, prices fall and LM shifts right.

How fast prices move divides the schools:
- **Classical**: prices adjust fast; the economy is always near FE. Money is neutral even in the short run.
- **Keynesian**: prices are **sticky** (menu costs, contracts), so for a while output is set by demand where IS and LM cross, and FE is reached only slowly.

## A monetary expansion
The central bank raises $M$. LM shifts right: $r$ falls and, with prices sticky, $Y$ rises above FE. Over time prices rise, LM shifts back, and output returns to $\bar{Y}$ with a higher $P$: money is **neutral in the long run**, but not in the short run (Keynesian view).

```graph
# A monetary expansion, prices sticky
axes: Y | r
IS: down
LM: up
FE: vertical 0.5
shift LM: right
equilibrium: IS, LM
```

## A fiscal expansion
Government purchases rise: IS shifts right. In the short run $Y$ and $r$ both rise; in the long run prices rise until LM meets IS on FE, at a **higher real rate** and the same output: investment and consumption are crowded out.

## Investment in numbers
- $K_1 = 1000$, $d = 5\%$, $I_1 = 80$: next period's capital is {{1030|how much?}}.
eq: Capital accumulation | K_2 = (1-d)\,K_1 + I_1
- Firms want $K_2^* = 1100$; with $K_1 = 1000$ and $d = 5\%$ they invest {{150|how much?}}.
eq: Optimal investment | I_1 = K_2^* - (1-d)\,K_1
- A machine costs 10,000, $r = 3\%$, $\delta = 10\%$: its user cost is {{1300|how much?}} a year.
eq: User cost of capital | uc = p_K\,(r + \delta)
- Same machine: if the real rate rises to 5%, the user cost rises to {{1500|how much?}}, so fewer machines pass the test.
eq: User cost of capital | uc = p_K\,(r + \delta)
- An investment tax credit that lowers the effective price of capital shifts the investment curve {{right|which way?}}.

## Tracing shocks
- A temporary shock that raises the price of a country's exports works like a temporary rise in {{productivity|what?}}: saving shifts right.
- After a temporary productivity shock in a closed economy, investment rises because the real interest rate {{falls|rises or falls?}}, a movement along the curve.
- After a permanent productivity shock, employment rises less than after a temporary one, because workers feel richer and labor supply shifts {{left|which way?}}.
- After a permanent productivity shock, saving doesn't shift, because consumption rises by about the full rise in {{income|what?}}.
- A temporary shock raises a small open economy's current account; a permanent one {{lowers|raises or lowers?}} it.
- A small open economy whose saving shifts right far enough can switch from a current account deficit to a {{surplus|what?}}.
- The extra capital built today raises future output a little, but the change in today's consumption from that is small enough to {{ignore|what can we do?}}.

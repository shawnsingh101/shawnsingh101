## Numbers in the market for skill
- With $\alpha = 0.4$ and a skill premium of 2, firms want $S/L = \tfrac{0.4}{0.6}\times\tfrac{1}{2} \approx$ {{0.33|what ratio?}}.
eq: Relative demand for skill | \frac{S}{L} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{w_L}{w_S}
- With $\alpha = 0.5$ and a fixed supply of one skilled unit per low-skilled unit ($S/L = 1$), the equilibrium premium is {{1|what number?}}.
eq: Relative demand for skill | \frac{S}{L} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{w_L}{w_S}
- With an elasticity of substitution of 1, a 10% rise in the premium leaves the skilled wage bill relative to the low-skilled one {{unchanged|what happens to it?}}.
- Skilled pay is 60 dollars an hour and low-skilled 25: the premium is {{2.4|what number?}}.
eq: Wage skill premium | \frac{W_S}{W_L} = \frac{w_S}{w_L}

## Both sides of the border
- Low-skilled emigration from a poor country shifts its relative supply of skill {{right|which way?}}, so its premium falls.
- Migration could raise inequality in poor countries too if mostly the {{skilled|which workers?}} leave, because moving is costly.
- Trade liberalization makes a poor country specialize in {{low-skill-intensive|what kind of?}} goods, which lowers its premium.
- Offshoring raises demand for skill in poor countries too, because the stages they receive are {{skill-intensive|what?}} by their own standards.
- Besides offshoring, the force that fits rising premiums in rich and poor countries alike is {{skill-biased technical change|what?}}.

## Monopsony
- A monopsonist's marginal cost of labor is above the wage, because hiring one more worker means paying {{everyone|whom?}} more.
- With a labor supply elasticity of 4, the markdown $MPN/w$ is {{1.25|what number?}}.
eq: Monopsony markdown | \frac{MPN}{w} = 1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}
- Compared with perfect competition, a monopsonist hires {{fewer|more or fewer?}} workers.
- As labor supply becomes perfectly elastic, the markdown ratio goes to {{1|what number?}}.
eq: Monopsony markdown | \frac{MPN}{w} = 1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon}

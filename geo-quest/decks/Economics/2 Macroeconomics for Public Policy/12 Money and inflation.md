What money is, how central banks create it, the quantity theory, why sustained inflation is a monetary phenomenon, money demand, and what governments gain from printing money. Lecture 12 of Macroeconomics for Public Policy.

:::lesson
## What money does
Money is a **medium of exchange** (it ends barter's need for a "double coincidence of wants"), a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it), and a **store of value**. **M1** counts currency and checking deposits; **M2** adds savings deposits and small time deposits.

## Who creates it
The central bank controls the **monetary base**: currency plus banks' reserves at the central bank. It changes it mainly through **open-market operations**: buying bonds pays for them with new reserves. Banks lend out part of their deposits, which come back as new deposits, so the money supply is a multiple of the base.

## The quantity theory
Every dollar spent is a dollar received:

$$MV = PY$$

If velocity is stable and output is set by the supply side, more money means proportionally higher prices. In growth rates:

$$\pi = g_M + g_V - g_Y$$

Milton Friedman: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." In the long run money is **neutral**: it changes prices, not real output. The **Fisher effect**: higher money growth raises expected inflation, and the nominal rate rises one for one ($i = r + \pi^e$).

## Money demand
People hold money for convenience but give up interest: real money demand $L(Y, i)$ **rises with income** (more transactions) and **falls with the nominal rate** (the cost of holding money).

## Printing money to pay the bills
New money buys goods for the government: **seigniorage**, $\Delta M / P$. Inflation acts as a tax on money holders (the **inflation tax**). Push it too far and people hold less money, so the tax base shrinks and the government prints faster still: **hyperinflation**, as in Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2008 and Venezuela after 2016.

## Why inflation hurts
Menu costs, the "shoe-leather" cost of holding less money, a tax system written in nominal terms, unexpected transfers from lenders to borrowers, and noise in prices that hides real signals.

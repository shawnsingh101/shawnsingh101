title: Unemployment, inflation and the Phillips curve
---
The trade-off between inflation and unemployment, why it broke down in the 1970s, expectations, Okun's law, and what it takes to bring inflation down. Lecture 15 of Macroeconomics for Public Policy.

:::lesson
## The original curve
In 1958 A. W. Phillips found that in Britain, years of low unemployment were years of fast wage growth. Policymakers in the 1960s read it as a **menu**: pick a bit more inflation, get a bit less unemployment.

## Expectations
Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps warned (1967–68) that the trade-off holds only while inflation is **unexpected**. Workers and firms build expected inflation into wages and prices:

$$\pi = \pi^e - h\,(u - \bar{u})$$

Unemployment falls below its **natural rate** $\bar{u}$ only when inflation beats expectations. Once people expect the higher inflation, unemployment returns to $\bar{u}$ at the higher inflation rate. In the long run the Phillips curve is **vertical** at $\bar{u}$. The 1970s proved them right: inflation and unemployment rose together.

The natural rate is the unemployment left when the economy is at full employment: **frictional** (people between jobs) plus **structural** (skills or places that don't match the jobs). Unemployment above it is **cyclical**.

## Okun's law
Each point of cyclical unemployment costs about two percent of output:

$$\frac{\bar{Y} - Y}{\bar{Y}} = 2\,(u - \bar{u})$$

## Bringing inflation down
To lower inflation, actual inflation must fall below expected, which means unemployment above $\bar{u}$ for a while. The total cost is the **sacrifice ratio**: output lost per point of inflation removed. Paul Volcker's Fed took US inflation from about 13% in 1980 to about 4% by 1983, at the cost of a deep recession and unemployment near 11%.

The cost is lower when the central bank is **credible**: if people believe the disinflation, expectations fall fast and less unemployment is needed. That's the case for **central bank independence** and **inflation targets** (2% in the US, the euro area and the UK).

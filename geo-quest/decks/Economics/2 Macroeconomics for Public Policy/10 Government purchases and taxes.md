:::lesson
## The government's budget constraint
A government, like a household, can borrow, but it must pay back in present value. With no debt to start with:

$$G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+r} = T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+r}$$

Cutting taxes today, with spending unchanged, means raising them later by the cut plus interest.

## Ricardian equivalence
Households face the same constraint once the government's is folded into theirs: what matters for their lifetime wealth is the present value of **taxes**, which equals the present value of **government purchases**. So a tax cut today, paid for by borrowing, leaves lifetime wealth unchanged. Forward-looking households **save the whole cut** to pay the future tax, and consumption doesn't move. National saving is unchanged: private saving rises by exactly what public saving falls. This is **Ricardian equivalence** (after David Ricardo; revived by Robert Barro).

It fails when households are **credit-constrained** (they spend a tax cut they couldn't borrow), **short-sighted**, or expect the future tax to fall on someone else (the next generation), or when taxes are **distortionary**, not lump-sum.

## More government purchases
A **temporary** rise in $G_1$ uses goods now. Households feel poorer (they'll pay for it in present value) and cut consumption, but by less than $G_1$ rises, since they smooth the loss over both periods. So **national saving falls**: saving shifts left.

```graph

S: up
I: down
shift S: left
```

- **Closed economy**: the real rate rises and investment falls: **crowding out**.
- **Small open economy**: the rate stays at $r_W$; investment is unchanged, and the current account moves toward **deficit**. A budget deficit beside a current account deficit is the **twin deficits** story.

## Taxes that change choices
A lump-sum tax changes wealth but not prices. A tax on wages changes the price of leisure: the worker keeps $w(1-t)$, and the gap is the **tax wedge**. It lowers labor supply (the substitution effect) and makes people poorer (the income effect, which works the other way). Taxes on capital income lower the return to saving. Every distortionary tax has a **deadweight loss** that grows roughly with the **square** of the tax rate, which is why spreading taxes evenly over time (**tax smoothing**) beats raising them sharply in some years.

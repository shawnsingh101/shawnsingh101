## Three curves, again
With output $Y$ across and the price level $P$ up:
- **AD** (aggregate demand): where IS and LM cross for each price level. A higher $P$ lowers real money, shifts LM left and lowers output, so AD **slopes down**. Anything that shifts IS or LM right (more government spending, more money) shifts AD right.
- **LRAS** (long-run aggregate supply): vertical at full-employment output $\bar{Y}$.
- **SRAS** (short-run aggregate supply): with sticky prices, **horizontal** at the current price level: firms meet demand at their posted prices for a while.

```graph

axes: Y | P
AD: down
SRAS: horizontal 0.45
LRAS: vertical 0.5
shift AD: right
equilibrium: AD, SRAS
```

## A demand shock
AD shifts right: in the short run output rises above $\bar{Y}$ at the same prices. Firms then raise prices, SRAS moves up, and output returns to $\bar{Y}$ at a higher price level. A fall in demand runs the other way: a **recession** now, falling prices later.

## A supply shock
An oil price jump or a fall in productivity shifts **LRAS left**. Output falls and prices rise together: **stagflation**, as in 1973–74 and 1979.

## Should policy lean against the wind?
If prices are sticky, a central bank or government can shift AD back after a demand shock and shorten a recession: **stabilization policy**. Against it: **lags** (recognizing the problem, acting, and the effect each take months), uncertainty about where $\bar{Y}$ is, and political temptation. A supply shock is harder still: pushing AD right fights the output loss but adds to inflation.

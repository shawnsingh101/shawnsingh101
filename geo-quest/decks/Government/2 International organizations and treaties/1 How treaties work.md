## What a treaty is
A **treaty** is a written agreement between countries (or international organizations) that is binding under international law. Its name doesn't matter: convention, pact, protocol, charter, covenant and accord can all be treaties. The rulebook for treaties is itself a treaty, the **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties** (1969, in force 1980). Its core rule is old Latin: *pacta sunt servanda*, "agreements must be kept".

## From handshake to law
```flow
Negotiators | Governments | Depositary
Negotiators: agree a text
Governments: sign it (intent; don't undermine it)
Governments: ratify it at home (consent to be bound)
Governments -> Depositary: deposit the instrument of ratification
Depositary: count ratifications; the treaty enters into force
```
**Signing** is not yet being bound: it shows intent and creates a duty not to defeat the treaty's purpose. **Ratification** is the act that binds. A country that joins later, without having signed, **accedes**. A **reservation** opts out of a clause when joining, if the treaty allows it. A **protocol** is an add-on treaty (the Kyoto Protocol to the climate convention).

## The United States
- **Article II**: the President "shall have Power, by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties, provided two thirds of the Senators present concur". The Senate does not ratify; it consents, and the **President** ratifies.
- **Article VI**, the Supremacy Clause: treaties are "the supreme Law of the Land", equal to federal statutes; a later statute can override one at home (the *last-in-time* rule).
- Most US international agreements are **not** Article II treaties: **congressional-executive agreements** pass by simple majorities of both houses (NAFTA, the USMCA, WTO membership), and **sole executive agreements** rest on the President's own powers.
- **Self-executing** treaties work as law at once; non-self-executing ones need Congress to pass a law first (*Medellín v. Texas*, 2008).
- **Leaving**: most treaties say how (NATO: one year's notice; Paris: one year's notice). Presidents have withdrawn on their own authority, and the Supreme Court has never settled whether Congress must agree (*Goldwater v. Carter*, 1979).

The Senate's most famous no: the **Treaty of Versailles**, rejected in 1919 and 1920, so the US never joined the League of Nations.

- A treaty starts to bind on the day it {{enters into force|does what?}}, often once a set number of countries have ratified it.
- The steps of a multilateral treaty
```parts
Diplomats agree the wording :: negotiate
Show intent, not yet bound :: sign
Consent to be bound, at home :: ratify
Hand the paper to the keeper :: deposit
Enough countries have joined :: enter into force
```

Why the Constitution was written, how the Philadelphia convention made it, how it was ratified, and how its seven articles fit together. Written from the National Archives, the text itself and the Library of Congress's Constitution Annotated (public domain).

:::lesson
## Why a new constitution
The first national charter, the **Articles of Confederation** (in force 1781), made a weak league of states: Congress could not tax, regulate trade or raise an army on its own, and changing the Articles needed every state. **Shays' Rebellion** of Massachusetts farmers (1786–87) frightened leaders into acting.

## The Philadelphia convention
Delegates met in the Pennsylvania State House from **May 25 to September 17, 1787**, with **George Washington** presiding, in secret. **James Madison**, the "Father of the Constitution", brought the **Virginia Plan** (a strong national government, both houses by population); small states answered with the **New Jersey Plan** (one state, one vote). The **Great (Connecticut) Compromise**, pushed by Roger Sherman, gave the House seats by population and the Senate two per state. The **Three-Fifths Compromise** counted three of every five enslaved people for seats and taxes. **Gouverneur Morris** wrote the final wording, including "We the People". **39** delegates signed.

## Ratification
Article VII needed **nine of thirteen** state conventions. **Delaware** ratified first (December 7, 1787); **New Hampshire** was the ninth (June 21, 1788); Virginia and New York followed after hard fights, and Rhode Island came last (1790). To win them over, Hamilton, Madison and John Jay wrote the **Federalist Papers** (85 essays signed "Publius"); the **Anti-Federalists** wanted a bill of rights, which supporters promised. The new government began on **March 4, 1789**.

## The seven articles
```org
# The Constitution
Preamble | "We the People…": six purposes
  Article I | the legislature: Congress
  Article II | the executive: the President
  Article III | the judiciary: the Supreme Court
  Article IV | the states and each other
  Article V | amending
  Article VI | debts, supremacy, oaths
  Article VII | ratification
```
Three big ideas run through it: **separation of powers** (three branches), **checks and balances** (each can block the others), and **federalism** (power split between the nation and the states). It is the oldest written national constitution still in use.

- Each branch's power to block the others is called {{checks and balances|what?}}.
- Dividing power between the national government and the states is {{federalism|what principle?}}.
- Congress can override a presidential veto by a {{two-thirds|what share?}} vote of both houses.

title: Who runs foreign policy
---
How the Constitution splits foreign affairs between the President and Congress: treaties, ambassadors, war, money, and the agreements that never go to the Senate.

:::lesson
## An invitation to struggle
The Constitution never says who is in charge of foreign policy. It hands pieces to each branch, which is why the scholar Edward Corwin called it "an invitation to struggle".

**The President (Article II)**
- is **commander in chief** of the armed forces;
- **makes treaties**, with the advice and consent of the Senate;
- **nominates ambassadors**, with the Senate's consent;
- **receives ambassadors**, which carries the power to recognize foreign governments. The Supreme Court confirmed in *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015) that recognition belongs to the President alone.

**Congress (Article I)**
- **declares war** and raises and funds the armed forces;
- **regulates foreign commerce**: tariffs and trade;
- holds the **power of the purse**: no embassy opens and no aid is spent without money it appropriates;
- defines offenses against the law of nations;
- the **Senate** confirms ambassadors, the Secretary of State and other senior officials, and approves treaties.

In practice the President leads day to day, and Congress shapes policy through money, laws, confirmations and oversight.

## How a treaty takes effect
A common mistake is to say the Senate ratifies treaties. It doesn't: it gives **advice and consent**, and the President ratifies.

```flow
# How a treaty takes effect
President | Senate | Other country
President -> Other country: negotiates and signs the treaty
President -> Senate: sends it with a message asking for consent
Senate: the Foreign Relations Committee holds hearings and reports a resolution of ratification
Senate: two thirds of the senators present vote to consent
President -> Other country: ratifies and exchanges or deposits the instruments of ratification
```

## Agreements that aren't treaties
Most international agreements the US makes are **executive agreements**, not treaties: well over nine in ten. Some rest on an act of Congress (congressional-executive agreements, like most trade deals), some on the President's own powers. The **Case-Zablocki Act** (1972) requires the State Department to report them to Congress, so they can't be kept secret from it.

## War powers
After Vietnam, Congress passed the **War Powers Resolution** (1973) over President Nixon's veto. The President must notify Congress within **48 hours** of sending forces into hostilities, and withdraw them within **60 days** (plus 30 to pull out) unless Congress authorizes it. Presidents of both parties have called it unconstitutional, and it has rarely been enforced.

## The committees
- **Senate Foreign Relations Committee**: treaties and nominations for State.
- **House Foreign Affairs Committee**: the House's counterpart.
- **Appropriations subcommittees** in each chamber write the yearly bill that funds State and foreign aid.

## Private diplomacy
The **Logan Act** (1799) makes it a crime for a private citizen to negotiate with a foreign government over a dispute with the US without authorization. Only two people have ever been indicted under it, the last in 1852, and no one has been convicted.

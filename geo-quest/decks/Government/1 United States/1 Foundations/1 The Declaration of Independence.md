## How it came about
Fighting with Britain had begun at Lexington and Concord in April 1775, but for a year most colonists still hoped to make peace with the King. Thomas Paine's pamphlet **Common Sense** (January 1776) helped change that. On **June 7, 1776**, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moved in the Second Continental Congress, meeting in the Pennsylvania State House in Philadelphia (now Independence Hall), that "these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States".

Congress named a **Committee of Five** to write a declaration explaining the break: **Thomas Jefferson**, **John Adams**, **Benjamin Franklin**, **Roger Sherman** and **Robert R. Livingston**. Jefferson, 33, wrote the draft; Adams and Franklin made small changes. Congress voted for independence on **July 2** (the Lee Resolution) and adopted the Declaration's text, after cutting about a quarter of it, on **July 4**. Among the cuts: Jefferson's passage blaming the King for the slave trade.

That night John Dunlap printed about 200 copies (the **Dunlap broadsides**). The handwritten, **engrossed** copy on parchment was signed mostly on **August 2, 1776**, by **56** delegates, John Hancock, the President of Congress, first and largest. It is in the Rotunda of the **National Archives** in Washington.

## Its five parts
```stack
# The Declaration, top to bottom
1 | Introduction | "When in the Course of human events…": a people owe the world their reasons
2 | Preamble | "We hold these truths to be self-evident…": the theory of government
3 | Indictment | 27 grievances against King George III
4 | Denunciation | the British people were warned too, and didn't listen
5 | Conclusion | the colonies "are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States"
```

## What it did
The vote of July 2 made the break; the Declaration **explained** it, to Americans, to Britain and to France, whose help the colonies needed. It is not law: it set up no government (the Articles of Confederation and then the Constitution did). Its preamble, borrowing from **John Locke** and George Mason's **Virginia Declaration of Rights**, gave the country its creed: rights come from nature, not from rulers; governments get their power from **the consent of the governed**; and a people may alter or abolish a government that destroys their rights. Lincoln built the Gettysburg Address on it, and the abolitionists and the Seneca Falls women's rights convention (1848) quoted it back at the country.

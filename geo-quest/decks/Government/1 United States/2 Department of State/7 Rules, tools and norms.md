title: Rules, tools and norms
---
How the department works inside: its rulebook, cables and clearances, the formal tools of diplomacy, and the laws that limit what it can do.

:::lesson
## The rulebook
The **Foreign Affairs Manual** (FAM) holds the department's official regulations and policies, with step-by-step procedures in the **Foreign Affairs Handbooks** (FAH). It's organized in volumes (personnel, consular affairs, visas, diplomatic security…) and most of it is public. When someone says "it's in the FAM", they mean it's the rule.

## How paper moves
Washington and its posts talk by **cable**: formal, numbered messages in a set format, from the Secretary (outgoing cables go out over the Secretary's name) or from the ambassador. A post's reporting (on an election, a meeting, an economy) arrives as cables read across the government. Before any document leaves an office it must be **cleared**: every office with a stake signs off. Clearance is slow, but it's how the department speaks with one voice.

## The tools of diplomacy
- A **démarche** is a formal approach to a foreign government to deliver a position or a request, usually on instructions from Washington.
- A **diplomatic note** is a formal written message between a foreign ministry and an embassy, written in the third person. The most common kind is the **note verbale**.
- **Recognition** is a decision about a government or a state; **diplomatic relations** are a separate step. Relations can be cut or suspended without withdrawing recognition.
- When two countries have no relations, a third country can act as **protecting power**: Switzerland has long represented US interests in Iran.
- Ambassadors in a capital rank by the date they presented their credentials; the longest-serving is the **dean of the diplomatic corps**.

## Laws that limit
- The **Leahy Law** bars US security assistance to any unit of a foreign security force when there is credible information that it committed a gross violation of human rights.
- The **Arms Export Control Act** governs arms sales; Congress must be notified of major sales and can try to block them. The rules on exporting weapons and military technology are the **International Traffic in Arms Regulations** (ITAR), run by State's Political-Military bureau.
- The **Hatch Act** limits partisan political activity by federal employees.
- Gifts from foreign governments worth more than a small amount belong to the **United States**, not to the official who received them.

## Information
Classified information has three levels: **Confidential**, **Secret** and **Top Secret**. Much of State's unclassified work is marked **Sensitive But Unclassified**. And State publishes the official documentary history of US foreign policy, the **Foreign Relations of the United States** (FRUS) series, through its Office of the Historian.

---
title: Embassies and consulates
---
How a US mission abroad works: the ambassador and the country team, the sections and the agencies inside, the rules that protect diplomats, and what happens when things go wrong.

:::lesson
## Posts
A **post** is any US office abroad. The **embassy** is in the capital and handles relations with the government. **Consulates general** and **consulates** in other cities serve Americans and visa applicants and report on their region. All of them in one country make up the **mission**. Missions to international organizations (the US Mission to the UN in New York, to NATO in Brussels) are posts too. The building itself is the **chancery**; the ambassador's home is the **residence**.

## The chief of mission
The **ambassador** is the President's personal representative, accredited to the head of state. As **chief of mission** they direct every executive branch employee in the country (from State, Defense, Commerce, the FBI…), except troops under a military combatant commander. Each new ambassador gets a **letter of instruction** from the President spelling this out.

Before an ambassador is named, the host government is asked for its **agrément**, its consent. On arrival, the ambassador presents a **letter of credence** to the head of state. When the ambassador is away or the post is vacant, the officer in charge is the **chargé d'affaires**.

The **deputy chief of mission** (DCM) is the second in command and runs the embassy day to day, like a chief operating officer.

## The country team
The ambassador leads a **country team**: the heads of each section and of every other agency at post, meeting regularly so the US speaks with one voice.

The State sections line up with the five Foreign Service career tracks:
- **Political**: reports on and influences the host government, politics, human rights.
- **Economic**: trade, investment, energy, commercial disputes.
- **Consular**: visas, and **American Citizen Services**: passports, births, deaths, arrests, emergencies.
- **Management**: the platform everyone runs on: people, money, housing, facilities, IT.
- **Public diplomacy** (the public affairs section): press, social media, cultural and educational exchanges.

Others at post often include:
- the **regional security officer** (RSO), from Diplomatic Security;
- a **Marine Security Guard** detachment, whose main job is protecting classified material;
- the **defense attaché** and a security cooperation office, from Defense;
- the Foreign Commercial Service (Commerce), the Foreign Agricultural Service (Agriculture), the FBI's legal attaché, and others.

Most of an embassy's staff are **locally employed staff**: host country citizens who give the mission its memory and language.

## The rules: the Vienna Conventions
The **Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations** (1961) sets the rules nearly every country follows:
- The mission's premises are **inviolable**: host authorities may not enter without permission, and must protect them. An embassy is *not* the sending country's territory; it is protected ground.
- Diplomats have **immunity** from the host country's criminal courts (and most civil ones). The sending country can waive it.
- The **diplomatic bag** may not be opened or detained.
- The host's remedy is to declare a diplomat **persona non grata**, at any time and without giving a reason, after which they must leave.

The **Vienna Convention on Consular Relations** (1963) covers consulates. Under it, when a foreigner is arrested, the authorities must tell them they may have their consulate notified: **consular notification**.

## When things go wrong
- An evacuation can be **authorized departure** (staff and families may leave) or **ordered departure** (they must).
- Attacks reshaped embassy security: the 1983 Beirut bombings led to the **Inman** standards (setbacks and walls), and the 1998 bombings in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam to a wave of new, fortified embassies. By law, an attack that kills or seriously injures someone at a post triggers an **Accountability Review Board**, as after Benghazi in 2012, where Ambassador Chris Stevens was killed.

---
title: The Foreign Service
---
Who does the work: the Foreign Service and its five career tracks, specialists, the Civil Service and local staff; ranks and promotion; how assignments work; and the laws that built the system.

:::lesson
## Three workforces
- The **Foreign Service** serves abroad and rotates: two or three years at a post, then a new one, with tours in Washington in between. Its members sign up for **worldwide availability**: they go where they're needed.
- The **Civil Service** mostly stays in Washington and other US offices, providing expertise and continuity: lawyers, analysts, passport specialists, budget officers.
- **Locally employed staff** are hired in each country, the largest group, and the institutional memory of every post.

## Officers and specialists
Foreign Service **generalists**, called **Foreign Service officers** (FSOs), each belong to one of five **career tracks**, informally called **cones**:
- **Consular**: visas, passports and help for Americans abroad; also fraud prevention and crisis response.
- **Economic**: trade, energy, the environment, science and technology, commercial advocacy.
- **Management**: running the post: people, budgets, facilities, logistics.
- **Political**: analyzing the host country, negotiating, reporting, human rights and security issues.
- **Public diplomacy**: explaining US policy to foreign publics: press, media, culture and exchanges.

Whatever their cone, most new officers serve at least one tour doing **consular** work, usually as a visa officer.

Foreign Service **specialists** bring a particular skill: Diplomatic Security special agents, information technology managers, medical officers, facility managers, office management specialists, and more.

## Ranks and promotion
Ranks run from **FS-6** up to **FS-1** (comparable to a colonel). Above that is the **Senior Foreign Service**, in four steps: **Counselor**, **Minister-Counselor**, **Career Minister**, and the rare honorary rank of **Career Ambassador**.

Promotion is decided by **selection boards** that read each officer's evaluations and rank them against their peers. The system is **up or out**: an officer who isn't promoted within a time limit for their rank (time in class) must leave. Retirement is mandatory at **65**.

## Assignments
Each assignment is **bid** on: officers list the jobs they want, and posts and bureaus pick. The first two tours, for entry-level officers, are **directed**: assigned by the department. Posts with harsh conditions pay a **hardship differential**; dangerous ones add **danger pay**. Some posts are **unaccompanied**: no families allowed.

## Getting in
The **Foreign Service Officer Test** is the start of a selection that also reviews candidates' written responses and backgrounds, then an assessment, then medical and security clearances. Successful candidates are ranked on a **register** and offered places in an orientation class called **A-100**, at the **Foreign Service Institute**, the department's training school in Arlington, Virginia, which also teaches some 70 languages. The process changes from time to time (it was revised in 2025), so the official site, careers.state.gov, is the place to check. Recruiters called **Diplomats in Residence** are posted at universities around the country.

## The laws that built it
- The **Rogers Act** (1924) merged the separate diplomatic and consular services into one **Foreign Service**, with entry by merit exam.
- The **Foreign Service Act of 1980** is today's framework: it created the Senior Foreign Service and set the rules on ranks, promotion and grievances.
- The **Director General of the Foreign Service** heads its personnel system, and by law is a current or former Foreign Service officer.

## Speaking up
The **Dissent Channel**, created in 1971 during the Vietnam War, lets any employee send a formal disagreement with policy to the Secretary's Policy Planning Staff, which must reply, and bars retaliation for using it.
